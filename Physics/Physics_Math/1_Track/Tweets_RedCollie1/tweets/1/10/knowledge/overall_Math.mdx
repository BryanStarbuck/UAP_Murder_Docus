## Section: 1964861677742264334.yaml — Lorentz Force Law (Faraday Disc)

### Equation 1: General Lorentz Force
  F = q(E + v × B)

Terms:
- F: force vector on charge carrier [N]
- q: charge of carrier (electrons: q = -e = -1.602 × 10^-19 C)
- E: electric field vector [V/m]
- v: velocity vector of charge carrier [m/s]
- B: magnetic field vector [T]
- ×: cross product (magnitude |v||B|sin θ, direction by right-hand rule)

### Equation 2: Specific Cross Products in Cylindrical Coordinates

Using cylindrical basis {r̂, φ̂, ẑ}:
  r̂ × ẑ = -φ̂
  φ̂ × ẑ = -r̂
  ẑ × r̂ = φ̂
  ẑ × φ̂ = -r̂    [correction: ẑ × φ̂ = r̂]

For radial current (v_r r̂) in axial field (B_z ẑ):
  F_azimuthal = q v_r B_z (r̂ × ẑ) = -q v_r B_z φ̂

This azimuthal force produces the motor torque τ = r × F.

### Equation 3: E×B Drift (Cyclotron Physics)
  v_drift = (E × B) / |B|²

Terms:
- v_drift: guiding center drift velocity [m/s] — same for all charge signs and masses
- E: electric field [V/m]
- B: magnetic field [T]
- |B|²: square of the magnetic field magnitude [T²]

Relationship: The E×B drift is perpendicular to both E and B. In the Faraday disc, the radial E and axial B produce an azimuthal drift, consistent with the disc rotation.

### Equation 4: Cyclotron Frequency
  ω_c = qB / m

For electrons (q = e = 1.602 × 10^-19 C, m = m_e = 9.109 × 10^-31 kg):
  ω_c = eB / m_e = 1.7588 × 10^11 × B  [rad/s]

For B = 1 T: ω_c = 1.76 × 10^11 rad/s ≈ 28 GHz
For B = 0.1 T: ω_c ≈ 2.8 GHz (microwave range)

### Equation 5: Cyclotron Radius
  r_c = m_e v_⊥ / (eB) = v_⊥ / ω_c

For thermal electrons with v_th = (2k_B T / m_e)^(1/2):
At T = 300 K: v_th = (2 × 1.38 × 10^-23 × 300 / 9.109 × 10^-31)^(1/2) ≈ 9.5 × 10^4 m/s

  r_c (B=0.1T) = 9.5 × 10^4 / (1.76 × 10^10) ≈ 5.4 × 10^-6 m ≈ 5.4 μm

## Section: 2029085302464365005.yaml — 3-Phase Drive and Multipole Ring Equations

### Equation 7: Three-Phase Current System
  I_U(t) = I_0 cos(ωt)
  I_V(t) = I_0 cos(ωt - 2π/3)
  I_W(t) = I_0 cos(ωt + 2π/3)   [equivalent: cos(ωt - 4π/3)]

### Equation 8: Rotating Field Vector from 3-Phase Coils

For coils at angular positions θ_k = 0, 2π/3, 4π/3 (k = U,V,W), each producing a field along its axis:
  B(t) = Σ_k I_k(t) × (cos θ_k x̂ + sin θ_k ŷ)

Expanding:
  B_x(t) = B_0[cos(ωt)cos(0) + cos(ωt - 2π/3)cos(2π/3) + cos(ωt + 2π/3)cos(4π/3)]
  B_y(t) = B_0[cos(ωt)sin(0) + cos(ωt - 2π/3)sin(2π/3) + cos(ωt + 2π/3)sin(4π/3)]

Using trigonometric identities:
  B_x(t) = (3/2)B_0 cos(ωt)
  B_y(t) = (3/2)B_0 sin(ωt)

Result: B(t) = (3/2)B_0 [cos(ωt) x̂ + sin(ωt) ŷ]
Magnitude: |B(t)| = (3/2)B_0 = constant (rotating field of fixed amplitude)

### Equation 9: Multipole Field of 16-Magnet Ring

For a ring of 2p alternating poles (p pole-pairs), the azimuthal component of the magnetic field inside the ring varies as:
  B_φ(r, θ) = B_p (r/R)^(p-1) sin(pθ)

where:
- R = ring radius
- p = number of pole-pairs = 8 (for 16 alternating magnets)
- θ = azimuthal angle
- B_p = amplitude at radius R

The torque on the central spinning magnet from the outer ring's field:
  τ = m × B(r=0, pole field contribution)

For a centered dipole m = m ẑ, the torque requires a field component in the XY plane at r=0. For p = 8, the field at r → 0 goes as r^7 → 0. This means the direct torque on the centered dipole from the 8-pole-pair ring is extremely weak at the center — the coupling requires the central magnet to be displaced radially or to have a distributed current loop comparable in size to the ring.

This may explain why Drew places the outer ring ABOVE the coils rather than below — the axial displacement creates a non-zero axial gradient ∂B_z/∂z at the central magnet's location, enabling a net axial force even for an on-axis dipole.

### Equation 10: Axial Force on Dipole in Gradient Field
  F_z = m_z × (∂B_z/∂z)

Terms:
- m_z: z-component of the central magnet's dipole moment [A·m²]
- ∂B_z/∂z: axial gradient of the z-component of the field at the central magnet's location [T/m]

For levitation (F_z = m_g, where m is magnet mass and g = 9.81 m/s²):
  ∂B_z/∂z = m_g / m_z

If m_z = 1 A·m² (typical for a 100g neodymium magnet) and m = 0.1 kg:
  ∂B_z/∂z = (0.1 × 9.81) / 1 = 0.981 T/m ≈ 1 T/m

This is a readily achievable field gradient with rare-earth magnets — passive levitation systems (Earnshaw's theorem excluded by spinning) can achieve this.

### Equation 11: Earnshaw's Theorem and the Necessity of Spin

Earnshaw's theorem states that no static configuration of point charges (or magnetic dipoles in free space) can produce a stable equilibrium against all perturbations. Formally: for the potential Φ satisfying Laplace's equation (∇²Φ = 0), there are no stable extrema of Φ in free space.

For magnetic levitation of a permanent magnet by another permanent magnet (both static):
  ∇²U = 0 → no stable equilibrium

The spin/counter-spin mechanism circumvents Earnshaw's theorem by introducing time-varying fields (rotating field from 3-phase coils) and gyroscopic stabilization from the spinning central magnet. The gyroscopic precession prevents the levitated magnet from flipping its orientation, and the time-averaged field from the rotating drive can produce an effective potential well along Z even though the instantaneous Laplace condition forbids it.
