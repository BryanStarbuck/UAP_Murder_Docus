The primary experimental claim for anomalous lift in this source batch:

> "The magnet 'jumps up like a rabbit'."
> "To get the most force by spinning magnetic levitation, place a central magnet close to the center of six 3-phase wire coils"

**Description of the effect:** A permanent magnet placed at (or near) the geometric center of a hexagonal array of 6 wire coils, when the coils are energized with 3-phase AC, undergoes rapid upward (Z-axis) displacement. The characterization "jumps up like a rabbit" implies a sudden, vigorous, non-gradual motion — inconsistent with a gentle fringe-field gradient force, which would produce a smooth acceleration.

**The XY-to-Z coupling mechanism (claimed):** The rotating magnetic field lies predominantly in the XY-plane (horizontal, parallel to the coil plane). The observed force is along Z (vertical, perpendicular to the coil plane). This represents a cross-plane coupling: XY-plane electromagnetic energy → Z-axis mechanical force.

**Classical explanation pathway (partial):**

For a 3-phase coil array with finite physical extent (finite wire length, finite coil radius), the fringe fields at the coil ends have Z-components. The Z-component of the fringe field B_z(r, z) creates a Z-gradient ∂B_z/∂z that acts on the magnetic dipole:

F_z = m_z ∂B_z/∂z

The sign of ∂B_z/∂z above vs. below the coil plane determines whether the force is upward or downward. If the central magnet is placed just below the plane of maximum B_z, it experiences an upward force.

**Quantitative estimate (classical):** For a solenoid-like coil of radius a and length L, the axial field is:
B_z(z) = (μ₀ n I / 2) [cos α₁ + cos α₂]

where α₁, α₂ are the half-angles subtended by the coil ends at the field point. The gradient ∂B_z/∂z is maximum near the coil ends.

Whether this gradient force is sufficient to produce a visible "jump" of the magnet depends on:
- Coil current I (not specified)
- Magnet mass m_phys and dipole moment m_z
- Spring constant of the magnet's mounting (if any)

The claim that the effect is strong enough to see as a rapid "jump" suggests either high current or an anomalously large force beyond the classical fringe-field calculation.
