**Two Sets of 3-Phase Coils Offset by 60 Degrees**

> "Probably two sets of 3-phase coils offset by 60 degrees. That geometry works very well, and makes any magnetic flywheel spin."

A 3-phase coil set (3 coils, 120° apart) generates a rotating magnetic field vector in the XY-plane. The angular velocity of the rotating field equals the angular frequency of the applied AC power (ω = 2πf). Adding a second identical 3-phase set offset by 60° doubles the spatial sampling of the rotating field, increasing the uniformity of the rotating B-field and reducing torque ripple on the rotor. The combined 6-coil arrangement (two sets of 3, offset 60°) is equivalent to a 6-phase rotating machine, producing a more uniform rotating magnetomotive force (MMF) wave.

The key claim is that this 6-coil geometry "makes any magnetic flywheel spin" — including permanent magnet flywheels, which would not respond this way to a static field but do respond to a rotating field via the torque:

τ = m × B_rotating

where **m** is the magnetic dipole moment of the flywheel magnets and **B_rotating** is the rotating field vector. For a permanent magnet rotor, the equilibrium is synchronous rotation (the magnet follows the field), but Drew notes the outer ring in his device spins *against* the field direction (counter-rotation), implying a more complex interaction.

**Optimal Configuration for Spinning Magnetic Levitation**

> "To get the most force by spinning magnetic levitation, place a central magnet close to the center of six 3-phase wire coils"

This is a positioning optimization statement. The central magnet is a single permanent magnet placed at the geometric center of the 6-coil array. At this position, the rotating magnetic field has maximum radial symmetry. The net torque on the central magnet is azimuthal (φ-direction) by symmetry — but Drew claims the observed force is axial (Z-direction, "jumps up like a rabbit").

Classical analysis: for a magnetic dipole moment **m** aligned along the Z-axis at the center of a rotating in-plane (XY) magnetic field, the torque is τ = m × B, which lies in the XY-plane, rotating the dipole toward the B-field direction. There is no net Z-axis force on a magnetic dipole in a uniform rotating field if the field has no Z-gradient. The claim of axial "jump" therefore implies either:
1. A Z-axis gradient of the rotating field at the magnet position (field lines curl toward the axis and have an axial component), OR
2. A non-classical coupling mechanism.

**Rotary Induction Motor — Lorentz Force and Right-Hand Rule**

> "Those six (or 15) outer coils apparently act like a 'rotary induction motor', using the right-hand-rule or Lorentz force."

Formal description: a rotary induction motor operates by the following mechanism:
1. Polyphase stator currents I_s(t) = I_0 cos(ωt + φ_k), k = 0, 1, 2... for each phase, produce a rotating MMF:
   F(θ, t) = F_peak · cos(θ - ωt)
   where θ is the azimuthal angle and ω is the electrical angular frequency.
2. The rotating MMF establishes a rotating airgap flux density B(θ, t).
3. In a conducting rotor, the relative motion between B and the rotor conductors induces EMF by Faraday's law:
   ε = -dΦ/dt
4. Induced rotor currents J_r then experience Lorentz force:
   F = J_r × B
5. Net torque τ = ∫ r × (J_r × B) dV drives rotor rotation.

In Drew's device, there is no conducting rotor — instead, permanent magnets couple directly to the rotating field via the torque on their dipole moments. This is more analogous to a permanent magnet synchronous motor (PMSM). The right-hand rule determines the direction of torque:
- If B_rotating is clockwise and m points along +Z, the torque drives the magnet to align with the rotating B → synchronous counter-clockwise rotation when viewed from above if B rotates CCW.
- Counter-rotation of the outer magnet ring (against the field) requires explanation beyond standard PMSM theory.

**Alternative (15-coil configuration):** Drew also mentions a 15-coil variant. 15 coils in 3-phase configuration would be 5 groups of 3 coils, each group 120° apart within the group, with groups spaced 24° apart. This produces a much higher spatial harmonic content in the MMF waveform, which could create multiple counter-rotating field harmonics capable of driving complex multi-speed rotor behavior.

**Spacetime Wormhole — Kruskal Coordinates Reference**

> "Entering a spacetime wormhole (Kruskal coordinates)"

Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates are the maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime coordinates, in which the metric takes the form:

ds² = (32G³M³/r) · e^(-r/2GM) · (-dT² + dX²) + r²dΩ²

where T and X are the Kruskal time and space coordinates, r is the Schwarzschild radial coordinate (defined implicitly by (1 - r/2GM) = (X² - T²)·e^(r/2GM)), and dΩ² is the solid angle element. In these coordinates, the Einstein-Rosen bridge (wormhole) connecting two asymptotically flat regions is fully revealed: the future event horizon is at T = X, the past horizon at T = -X, and the two exterior regions (ours and the other universe) are visible simultaneously.
