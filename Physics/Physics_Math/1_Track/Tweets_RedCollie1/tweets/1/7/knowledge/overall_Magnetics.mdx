### Rotating Magnetic Field (r.m.f.) Generation via Three-Phase Coil Arrays

**Three-phase current fundamentals as applied in Drew's device:**

Three sinusoidal currents with equal amplitude and mutual phase offsets of 120° (2π/3 radians):
  I_U(t) = I_0 sin(omega*t)
  I_V(t) = I_0 sin(omega*t + 2π/3)
  I_W(t) = I_0 sin(omega*t + 4π/3)

When these currents flow through coils arranged symmetrically around a central axis (at angular positions 0°, 120°, 240° for a 3-coil set; or at 0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300° for a 6-coil set with U-V-W-U-V-W assignment), they produce a rotating magnetic field vector whose magnitude is constant and whose direction rotates at angular velocity omega. This is the standard result from symmetric three-phase machine theory: B_rot = (3/2) * mu_0 * n * I_0 * e^(i*omega*t) in the complex plane representation, where n is turns per coil and the field rotates continuously.

**Coil wiring topology — series vs. parallel — and its effect on counter-spin:**

From page_007.json (tweet 2018898646163726780, note_tweet field):
"The key to making UFO-style counter-spin work, using a ring of 12 wire coils, given 3-phase AC power as U-V-W-U-V-W-U-V-W-U-V-W, is to wire all of the 'U' coils in series rather than in parallel. Like for all of the 'V' coils, and all of the 'W' coils."

The significance: series wiring forces all coils of the same phase to carry identical current (series connection enforces I_series = I_total), whereas parallel wiring allows current sharing that can produce unequal flux contributions if coil impedances differ. With series wiring, the rotating field is spatially uniform, ensuring that the rotor-field interaction is governed purely by the relative polarity arrangement of the rotor magnets rather than by current distribution irregularities.

**Two distinct rotor polarity responses to the same r.m.f.:**

1. Alternating rotor: magnets arranged N-S-N-S-N-S-N-S around the rotor circumference. This creates an alternating pole pattern that locks to the rotating field analogously to a synchronous motor, but in the COUNTER-rotation sense (spins opposite to the r.m.f. rotation direction). Experimental confirmation: outer ring of 8 N-S magnets in ZrO2 bearing spins anti-clockwise when 6 coils drive r.m.f. clockwise.

2. All-same-polarity rotor: magnets arranged N-N-N-N-N-N-N-N around the rotor circumference. This produces a monopole-like ring topology which interacts with the r.m.f. and spins WITH the rotating field direction (co-rotation).

This distinction is not predicted by standard induction motor theory. In conventional induction motor theory, a conducting rotor develops torque in the direction of the rotating field (for motor action). The counter-rotation of an alternating-pole rotor in Drew's experiments represents an anomalous interaction requiring further theoretical analysis.

**Magnetic flywheel geometry:**

The device uses two concentric magnetic flywheels in the same horizontal plane:
- Outer flywheel: ring of 8 alternating N-S magnets inside a large-bore non-magnetic bearing, driven by 6 outer coils.
- Inner flywheel: 70mm central permanent magnet on a vertical shaft (the "spinning top"), driven by 3 lower coils.

Both sets of coils are driven with 3-phase power from separate variable-frequency power supplies. The outer coils (6 coils, clockwise r.m.f.) drive the outer ring counter-clockwise. The inner coils (3 coils, clockwise r.m.f.) drive the inner magnet clockwise. Net result: outer ring and inner magnet spin in opposite directions simultaneously.

From 13_2034171356779364352_video.mdx: "The outer part goes anti-clockwise, the inner part goes clockwise, so both rotating magnetic fields are clockwise from the six and the three."

This means both coil sets produce a clockwise r.m.f., but the rotor responses differ: the outer alternating-pole ring counter-rotates (anti-clockwise), the inner central magnet co-rotates (clockwise), because the inner magnet's polarity arrangement differs from the outer ring's alternating pattern.

**Isolation requirement — non-magnetic bearings:**

Steel bearings are ferromagnetic (relative permeability mu_r >> 1; typical bearing steel: mu_r ~ 100-1000). A ferromagnetic bearing in proximity to the spinning magnets would be strongly attracted, producing braking forces and disrupting the rotor's free precession. ZrO2 (zirconia) is diamagnetic/non-magnetic (mu_r ≈ 1.0). Si3N4 (silicon nitride) is similarly non-magnetic. Both allow the magnets to rotate freely without magnetic interaction with the bearing race.

A commenter (2046492341687701548.yaml) raised the concern that a steel ring in the assembly acts as "an inductor that collapses your field" and prevents field centering. Drew confirmed the use of "non-magnetic stainless steel" for all structural elements.

### Lenz Induction Driving Liquid Metal — XY-Plane Rotation

Liquid gallium (Ga, atomic number 31; melting point 29.8°C; electrical conductivity ~3.7×10^6 S/m at room temperature) is placed above spinning N-S permanent magnets. The moving magnetic flux from the spinning magnets induces eddy currents in the gallium (by Faraday's law: nabla × E = -∂B/∂t). These induced eddy currents (J_eddy) interact with the applied magnetic field (B) via the Lorentz body force: f = J × B (force per unit volume). The result is that the gallium is dragged into rotation by the magnets.

Transcription: "Liquid gallium being induced to spin by the magnets below and draw a big disc-like shape. Very interesting isn't it? Liquid gallium swirling in a vortex over these spinning N-S magnets."

The gallium forms a disc-like rotating pool — the centrifugal force (omega^2 * r) pushes the fluid radially outward, creating a shallow disc morphology. When the magnet spin is stopped, the gallium rotation decelerates due to viscosity (dynamic viscosity of Ga at 30°C ~ 1.9 mPa·s, comparable to water) and the cessation of the driving Lorentz force.

Behavioral equivalence to solid aluminum: Drew notes that liquid gallium "will behave just like aluminium, but in a liquid state." This is the conducting non-magnetic analogy: in both cases (solid Al disc or liquid Ga), the induction mechanism is identical (Faraday induction → eddy currents → Lorentz force → drag torque). The liquid state allows visual observation of the rotational flow pattern.

### Counter-Rotating Magnetic Field Driving Liquid Metal — Method 1

A counter-rotating magnetic field (r.m.f. that reverses its rotation direction) applied to a conducting liquid (gallium or mercury) produces counter-rotation of the liquid via Lenz induction. The physical mechanism: as the driving field reverses rotation, the flux change direction reverses, inducing eddy currents in the opposite sense, producing a Lorentz torque that now drags the liquid opposite to the original rotation.

Drew describes this as requiring "a LOT of energy" and being "very messy, with liquid gallium rushing through a tube, past the central 'holes' of many successive wire coils." The practical geometry appears to be a toroidal flow path through a tube that passes through multiple coil bores in sequence — a linear induction pump configuration applied to liquid metal. This is related to existing magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) pump designs used in nuclear reactor coolant loops with liquid sodium.

For liquid mercury in a cylindrical vessel with axial B_z and radial current (from central electrode to rim), the mercury rotates azimuthally.
