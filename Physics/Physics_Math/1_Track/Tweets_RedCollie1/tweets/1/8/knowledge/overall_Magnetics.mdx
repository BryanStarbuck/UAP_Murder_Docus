## Lorentz Force and the Liquid Metal Vortex

From tweet 1967608293947548075 (note_tweet): "In the left-hand picture, we can see a liquid-metal vortex spinning by the Lorentz force."

The Lorentz force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field is:
**F = q(E + v × B)** or in bulk form **f = J × B** (force density, N/m³)

For the liquid metal vortex: the rotating magnetic field B(t) induces eddy currents J in the liquid metal by Faraday induction: **∇ × E = -∂B/∂t**, and by Ohm's law **J = σ(E + v × B)**. The resulting J × B force drives azimuthal flow (vortex). The direction of vortex rotation relative to the driving field rotation is governed by Lenz's law: the induced current pattern creates a force that opposes the relative motion between field and conductor, meaning the vortex rotates in the direction that would reduce the apparent rotation of the field relative to the conductor — i.e., the conductor tends to co-rotate with the field. Counter-rotation in the liquid metal therefore requires that the field be made to rotate in the opposite direction, or that the geometry forces an inversion.

From 1964836025991406000.yaml: "We may have to apply 3-phase power to a Faraday disk to achieve UFO levitation. It has an induced magnetic field when in rapid spin."

The **Faraday disk** (homopolar generator) is a conducting disk rotating in an externally applied axial magnetic field B₀. Due to the motional EMF (v × B, where v is the tangential velocity of the conductor), an EMF is generated radially. If current flows radially through the disk (via brushes at center and rim), the rotating disk in the magnetic field acts as a DC generator (homopolar generator). 

In the inverse regime (motor operation): applying a DC current radially through the disk in an external axial field causes the disk to rotate (homopolar motor). The torque is T = (B₀ · I · (R₂² - R₁²))/2, where I is the radial current, R₁ and R₂ are inner and outer radii.
