### Gyroscopic Precession — Standard vs. Magnetic Top

Standard top mechanics: precession direction = same as spin direction.

The gyroscopic precession equation is:
    Ω_p = τ / L = (r × mg) / (I ω)

where:
- Ω_p = precession angular velocity (vector)
- τ = torque = r × F (gravitational torque)
- L = Iω = angular momentum
- r = position vector from pivot to center of mass
- m = mass, g = gravitational acceleration
- I = moment of inertia, ω = spin angular velocity

The direction of Ω_p follows from the right-hand rule applied to τ and L. For a
standard top spinning clockwise (viewed from above) and tilting under gravity,
precession is clockwise (same direction as spin).

Magnetic top claim: precession goes OPPOSITE to the spin direction. The additional
magnetic torque on the spinning magnetized top is:
    τ_mag = m_magnetic × B

where m_magnetic is the magnetic dipole moment of the top and B is the external
field (Earth's field or applied field). If τ_mag dominates over τ_gravity in
magnitude AND its cross-product orientation with L is antiparallel to τ_gravity's
cross-product with L, then the net precession direction reverses.

Condition for reversal: |τ_mag| > |τ_gravity| and the geometry is such that
Ω_p(net) = (τ_grav + τ_mag) / L has opposite sign to τ_grav / L alone.

Drew states this is "Never done before on Earth" — a first experimental
demonstration of magnetically-reversed precession direction.

### 3-Phase Rotating Magnetic Field Physics

Three-phase power produces a rotating magnetic field. For coils wound on a stator
at 0°, 120°, 240° with currents:
    I_U = I₀ cos(ωt)
    I_V = I₀ cos(ωt - 2π/3)
    I_W = I₀ cos(ωt - 4π/3)

The superposition produces a field vector rotating at angular frequency ω:
    B_x = B₀ cos(ωt)
    B_y = B₀ sin(ωt)

|B| = B₀ = constant (balanced 3-phase: no pulsation in amplitude)

Rotating field speed = ω/(pole_pairs). Reversing phase sequence (UVW → UWV)
reverses rotation direction.

For spin-and-counter-spin: two independent 3-phase systems with opposite phase
sequences create two co-axial rotating fields at ω and -ω. At any point in the
overlapping field region, the resultant field oscillates linearly rather than
circularly (two counter-rotating equal-amplitude circular polarizations = linear
polarization). However, at intermediate radii where one field dominates, a net
rotational component persists. The spatial gradient of the field creates a force on
induced-current-carrying conductors (Lorentz force) that varies with position.

### Lorentz Force Cancellation of Newton's Third Law

"The goal is to cancel Newton's 3rd Law using a Lorentz force. 3-phase force up,
but no 3-phase force down."

Physical mechanism: The Lorentz force on a charge carrier is F = q(v × B). For
a current-carrying conductor moving with velocity v_carrier in field B:

F_Lorentz = I (dl × B)

where dl is the vector element of the current path.

In the homopolar disc spinning at angular velocity ω_disc, current carriers have
both the radial component (from the applied DC current) and the azimuthal component
(from the rotation). The total carrier velocity:
    v_carrier = v_radial + v_azimuthal = v_r r̂ + ωr φ̂

The Lorentz force on these carriers in an axial field B_z:
    F = q(v_carrier × B_z ẑ)
      = q((v_r r̂ + ωr φ̂) × B_z ẑ)
      = q(v_r B_z (r̂ × ẑ) + ωr B_z (φ̂ × ẑ))
      = q(-v_r B_z φ̂ + ωr B_z r̂)

The radial component ωr B_z r̂ drives the homopolar torque. The azimuthal component
-v_r B_z φ̂ contributes to azimuthal force.

When the 3-phase field is applied, B is not purely axial but has a rotating
transverse component:

The Lorentz force in the rotating transverse field depends on the phase relationship
between the carrier velocity vector and the rotating B vector. Drew proposes that
