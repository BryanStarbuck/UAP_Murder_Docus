   The magnet rotates but stays near the bottom of the rod. Precessional regime

   resistance of approximately 1 kg_f at 20 Hz for 6 mm thick magnet.

   behavior. This confirms the effect is not pole-specific -- it depends on the
   magnitude of B and omega, not the orientation.

   3 mm magnet: rises, approximately 0.5 kg_f pushback at 20 Hz.
   6 mm magnet (double thickness): "almost impossible to turn it down now -- at least
   a kilogram of force pushing it up" at 20 Hz. Force scales approximately linearly
   with magnet thickness (linearly with magnet B-field strength).

8. Speed scaling:
   "The upward force is proportionate on how fast the magnet spins" -- upward force
   proportional to rotation frequency (omega).

GYROSCOPIC WEIGHT REDUCTION: Z-AXIS IMPLICATIONS
============================================================

Source (1954098493859270934.yaml note_tweet / 13_1954093865771859969_video.mdx):

"This also makes the 'heavy' gyroscope feel 'amazingly light', because so much of its
'weight' has turned sideways rather than vertical!"

Video observation (13_1954093865771859969): "It feels incredibly, incredibly light as I
do that." -- The experimenter is holding the spinning gyroscope and reporting a dramatic
reduction in perceived weight.

Physical mechanism: the gyroscopic precession torque redirects the gravitational force
vector from vertical (Z-axis) into horizontal (XY-plane) precessional motion. From
the perspective of supporting the gyroscope:
* Stationary gyroscope: support force = mg (full weight)
* Spinning gyroscope: support force < mg because the gravitational force is partially
  redirected into horizontal precession (the precession "absorbs" part of the gravity)

This is NOT a violation of Newton's Second Law in the standard sense -- the center of
mass still accelerates downward under gravity. However, the FORCE REQUIRED TO SUPPORT
the spinning gyroscope (hold it up) is reduced because the gyroscope is already
"using" some of the gravitational potential via precession.

The propulsion implication: a craft with internal high-speed gyroscopes (or equivalently,
high-speed spinning magnets in a magnetic field) would require less thrust to maintain
altitude, because the internal spin redirects a portion of gravitational force into
horizontal motion that can be further redirected.

PHASE TRANSITION IN LIFT: LOW TO HIGH RPM
============================================================

Source (1946435673562751159.yaml note_tweet, 13_1928757562629042176_video.mdx):

Two distinct regimes observed experimentally:

Regime 1 (low rpm, omega < omega_c):
* Magnet precesses in a wide horizontal circle (large XY-plane area)
* Small Z-axis displacement (low altitude)
* Described as: "precession at near-zero height"
* Corresponds to: w = h/a where h ~ 0, a large, w small

Regime 2 (high rpm, omega > omega_c):
* Magnet spins tightly around the vertical axis
* Large Z-axis displacement (magnet rises along pole)
* Described as: "spin at high height"
* Corresponds to: w = h/a where h large, a ~ 0, w large

Transition at omega_c ~ 15 Hz:
* System undergoes an abrupt change from Regime 1 to Regime 2
* Not a smooth continuous change -- the transition appears sudden
* Analogous to a topological phase transition (Berry phase change?) or a mechanical
  bifurcation (transition from precession orbit to spin-stabilized axial motion)

The critical frequency omega_c is set by the ratio of gravitational restoring force
to magnetic driving torque. Above omega_c, the magnetic driving torque overcomes gravity
and the gyroscopic effect stabilizes the axial spin.
