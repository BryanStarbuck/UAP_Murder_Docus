### Ring Magnet Focal Point — Magnetic Field Geometry

A toroidal (ring) permanent magnet — of the type used as a large loudspeaker magnet — possesses a **focal point** in the near-field interior of its bore. This focal point is a locus in three-dimensional space where the net force on a test dipole is zero in the radial direction while a finite restoring force exists along the axial direction, producing stable (or quasi-stable) levitation of the test dipole within the bore.

**Coordinate system**: Let the ring magnet lie in the XY-plane with the bore along the Z-axis. The field B(r,z) of the ring magnet has both radial B_r and axial B_z components. The force on a point magnetic dipole **m** at position (r, z) is:

**F** = ∇(**m** · **B**)

For a small permanent dipole aligned with the local field:

F_r = m · ∂B/∂r (radial component)
F_z = m · ∂B/∂z (axial component)

At the focal point r = r_f, z = z_f:
- F_r = 0 (radial force balance: repulsion from hole axis plus attraction toward ring bulk cancel)
- ∂F_r/∂r < 0 (restoring condition — negative stiffness in r provides the waggle oscillation)

The waggle behavior (damped oscillation observed experimentally) implies a local potential energy minimum in at least one spatial direction at the focal point. Note that Earnshaw's theorem prohibits a true local minimum of scalar magnetic potential for a free dipole in a static field; the quasi-stability observed is thus attributed to a saddle-point geometry where the axial restoring force overcomes the lateral instability on the relevant timescale.

**Physical significance**: The focal point provides a geometric constraint on the position of the inner spinning magnet relative to the outer ring magnet in the proposed propulsion device. The spinning magnet must occupy the focal point — it cannot sit on the central axis (field there is axially symmetric but radial force drives it outward) nor at the outer bore edge (no equilibrium), but exactly at r_f where repulsive and attractive gradient forces balance.

**Three-phase rotating field at the focal point**: Three wire coils at 120° angular separation are wound around the ring magnet assembly. Driven by three-phase AC at frequency f (experimentally demonstrated at approximately 12 Hz):

**B_rot(t)** = B_1 cos(2πft) x̂ + B_1 cos(2πft - 2π/3) ŷ + B_1 cos(2πft - 4π/3) ẑ_equiv

The vector sum produces a field of constant magnitude rotating at angular frequency ω = 2πf in the plane perpendicular to the Z-axis. This is the standard rotating stator field of a three-phase induction motor. When applied to the ring magnet system, it drives the small permanent magnet at the focal point into continuous rotation at or near the synchronous speed ω_s = 2πf (synchronous motor regime) or below it (induction regime with slip).

**Experimental observation**: At f ≈ 12 Hz, the inner magnet rotates continuously at the focal point. Removing three-phase power stops the rotation. Red tape marker used to visualize the rotation rate.

### XY-Plane and Z-Axis Magnetic Field Dynamics

**Configuration**: A permanent magnetic disc oriented at θ = 45° to the horizontal (XY) plane. Three wire coils at 120° angular separation apply three-phase AC, producing a rotating magnetic field **B_rot(t)** in the plane of the coils.

**Case 1 — On-axis, θ = 45°, disc spinning in-plane**:
The rotating field **B_rot** exerts a torque τ₁ = **m** × **B_rot** on the disc dipole **m**, driving it to spin about the disc normal axis (in-plane rotation). The same field, projected onto the perpendicular axis, exerts a secondary torque τ₂ attempting to flip the disc toward horizontal alignment. Gyroscopic inertia **L_gyro = I · ω** (moment of inertia I, spin angular velocity ω) resists τ₂. The disc cannot precess freely because the rigid mount constrains it. Net result: pure rotation about disc axis, no translation. No Z-axis lift.

**Case 2 — Off-axis, θ = 45°, disc offset by r from coil axis**:
When the disc center is displaced radially by r from the symmetry axis of the three-phase coil assembly:
- The rotating field drives disc spin as before
- The perpendicular torque τ₂ acts to precess the disc toward horizontal
- Gyroscopic resistance to τ₂ generates a gyroscopic reaction force perpendicular to both the spin axis and the precession axis
- With the disc offset, this gyroscopic reaction has a component along the Z-axis
- The disc straightens up AND translates upward by H

**H dependence**: H ∝ r² · f (derived from crop circle diagrams per Drew's account). The r² dependence suggests a second-order coupling — small offsets produce negligible lift, but lift grows rapidly with offset. The f-linear dependence indicates that higher three-phase frequency increases the torque magnitude and thus the gyroscopic reaction force linearly.

**Disc straightening dynamics**: The three-phase magnetic field rotates at ω = 2πf. The disc magnet spinning at angular velocity ω_spin experiences a gyroscopic angular momentum **L** = I · ω_spin · n̂ (n̂ = disc normal). The rotating field exerts a time-varying torque τ(t) on **L**. The gyroscopic precession rate is:

Ω_prec = |τ| / |L| = (m × B_rot) / (I · ω_spin)

For the off-axis case, this precession has a Z-component that accumulates into translational motion. The process is described as the disc "straightening up" (rotating from 45° toward 0°) while simultaneously translating upward — the two motions are coupled through the geometry of the offset.

**Summary of magnetic field roles**:
- XY-plane dynamics: rotating three-phase field drives spin, precession, and disc realignment
- Z-axis dynamics: offset + gyroscopic reaction produces net upward force H ∝ r² · f
- The magnetic field serves as the energy input; the vacuum serves as the reaction medium

### Dual Counter-Rotating Magnetic Field Configuration

**Inner rotor**: 70 mm diameter disc permanent magnet inside a non-magnetic (austenitic stainless steel) tube. Driven by three three-phase wire coils positioned below, with UVW winding. Rotates clockwise when viewed from above.

**Outer flywheel**: Plastic disc with permanent magnets arranged in alternating NS polarity around the circumference. Two configurations:
- 8-magnet flywheel: NS-NS-NS-NS (four NS pairs)
- 16-magnet flywheel: NS-NS × 8

The flywheel is driven by six external three-phase wire coils (UVW UVW) in series, producing a rotating magnetic field that drives the flywheel. The flywheel rotates on a Teflon paper bearing surface (selected to avoid the magnetic stiction problem encountered with austenitic steel ball-bearing races — even non-ferromagnetic stainless steel races caused excessive stiction due to the 70 mm magnet's field strength, preventing inner magnet rotation).

**Counter-rotation paradox**: Both inner (3-coil below) and outer (6-coil external) three-phase systems have their rotating magnetic fields set to "clockwise" in the phasor/field rotation sense. Yet the inner magnet rotates clockwise and the outer flywheel rotates anti-clockwise. This arises because:
- The inner coils are below the inner magnet: the field rotation seen by the inner rotor from below is clockwise in its reference frame
- The outer coils surround the outer flywheel: the field rotation seen by the outer rotor from outside is anti-clockwise in its reference frame (same physical field rotation, opposite mechanical coupling direction because the rotor is on the outside of the field, not the inside)

Result: Spin (inner, clockwise) + Counter-spin (outer, anti-clockwise) coexist in the same physical device.

**Upward force generation**: The two counter-rotating magnetic field systems interact to produce net upward translation of the inner magnet. The mechanism is described as analogous to the "jumping ring" electromagnetic induction demonstration — in the jumping ring, an AC field induces a current in a conducting ring and the induced-field/primary-field repulsion launches the ring upward. Here, the rotating magnetic fluxes from spin and counter-spin interact to produce an axial (Z-direction) force.

**Winding topologies**:
- Inner coils: 3-coil UVW, standard three-phase
- Outer coils: 6-coil UVW-UVW in series (two three-phase sets in series, producing stronger rotating field)

**Stainless steel tube material selection**: Non-magnetic (austenitic) grade required to avoid paramagnetic interaction with the disc magnet. Ferromagnetic or even weakly paramagnetic stainless grades (ferritic, martensitic) were found to grip the magnet and prevent free rotation/rise. Only austenitic (non-magnetic) grades allow the inner magnet to spin and rise freely.
