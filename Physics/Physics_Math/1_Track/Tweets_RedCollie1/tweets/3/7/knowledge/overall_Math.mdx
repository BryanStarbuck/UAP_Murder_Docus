### Ring Magnet Axial Field and Focal Point Equilibrium

**Axial magnetic field of a current loop (Biot-Savart):**

  B_z(z) = (μ_0 · I · R²) / (2 · (R² + z²)^(3/2))

where:
- μ_0 = 4π × 10^-7 T·m/A (permeability of free space)
- I = current in the loop [A]
- R = radius of the loop [m]
- z = axial distance from the loop center [m]

**Field gradient (first derivative):**

  ∂B_z/∂z = (μ_0 · I · R²) · (-3z) / (2 · (R² + z²)^(5/2))

The gradient is negative for z > 0 (field decreases with increasing z above the loop), zero at z = 0 (loop center), and positive for z < 0.

**Force on axial magnetic dipole m_z:**

  F_z = m_z · ∂B_z/∂z

For a permanent magnet with dipole moment m_z > 0 (aligned with B), this force is:
- Negative (downward, toward the loop) for z > 0: attractive
- Positive (upward, away from the loop) for z < 0: repulsive

**Equilibrium condition (focal point):**
For a ring magnet producing both attractive and repulsive contributions (due to its finite thickness and geometry, as opposed to a thin loop), the condition:

  F_z_total(z_f) = 0

defines the focal point z_f. Explicitly, for a thick ring magnet with inner radius R_1 and outer radius R_2:

  F_z(z) = m_z · [∂B_z(R_2,z)/∂z - ∂B_z(R_1,z)/∂z] = 0

Solved for z_f. Drew claims z_f ≈ 100 mm (0.1 m) above the ring coil center for his experimental geometry.

**Earnshaw's Theorem and the gyroscopic exception:**
Earnshaw's theorem (1842): No static configuration of charges or fixed magnets can provide stable equilibrium for a point charge or magnetic dipole in 3D space. The theorem applies to solutions of Laplace's equation ∇²φ = 0, which has no local extrema.

However, for a spinning magnetic dipole (gyroscope), Braunbek (1939) and later stabilization theory shows that the combination of magnetic gradient force and gyroscopic precession can create a stable orbit — Levitron-type stability. The stability condition requires:

  F_z(z_f) = 0   AND   ω_spin > ω_critical

where ω_critical depends on the field geometry and the mass/moment of inertia ratio of the spinning magnet.

### Gyroscope Precession Equation

Angular momentum:
  L = I · ω   [kg·m²/s]

Gravitational torque about the support point:
  |τ| = M · g · d   [N·m]

where d is the horizontal distance from the support point to the center of mass.

Precession angular velocity:
  Ω_p = τ / L = (M · g · d) / (I · ω)   [rad/s]

Precession period:
  T_p = 2π / Ω_p = 2π · I · ω / (M · g · d)   [s]

**Key relationship:** Ω_p ∝ 1/ω — faster spin → slower precession → more stable gyroscope.

**Nutation frequency:**
Small oscillations of the spin axis about the precession axis (nutation) occur at:
  Ω_nutation ≈ I · ω² / (I_perp · M · g · d)   [rad/s, high-spin approximation]

**Condition for gyroscopic stabilization preventing fall:**
The gyroscope does not fall when the precession is fast compared to the natural fall time t_fall = √(2h/g). The effective condition is:

  Ω_p · t_fall >> 1   →   (M·g·d)/(I·ω) · √(2h/g) >> 1

In the lab video (13_1940911383069020160), the spinning gyroscope is observed to balance on a string and appear to levitate — confirming that gyroscopic angular momentum is sufficient to redirect the gravitational torque into horizontal precession rather than downward fall.

- R_one = 1.1×10^-6 × 0.2 / 3.14×10^-6 ≈ 0.070 Ω

  P_nichrome = I² · R_nichrome = (43)² × 0.14 ≈ 259 W

This confirms nichrome "gets hot" as observed.

**MHD body force (Lorentz force density):**
  f = J × B   [N/m³]

For current density J = I/A_cross [A/m²] directed along ẑ through galinstan cross-section A_cross, in field B with radial component B_r from the permanent magnet below:

  f_φ = J_z · B_r   (azimuthal force driving rotation)

Total azimuthal force integrated over fluid volume V_fluid:
  F_φ = ∫∫∫ f_φ dV = I · ∫ B_r · (dz over path length)

This is equivalent to the force I·L×B for a straight conductor of length L in a uniform field B_r.

**Torque on the vortex:**
  τ_azimuthal = r_eff · F_φ

where r_eff is the effective moment arm of the current distribution. The angular acceleration of the liquid metal:

  α = τ / I_fluid   (I_fluid = moment of inertia of rotating fluid mass)

At steady state (terminal angular velocity Ω_steady), viscous drag τ_viscous = b·Ω_steady balances the driving torque:

  r_eff · I · B_r_eff = b · Ω_steady

So:   Ω_steady = (r_eff · I · B_r_eff) / b

To increase Ω_steady: increase I (by lowering R or raising V), or increase B_r_eff (by using stronger magnet or closer magnet placement).

### 3-Phase Motor Coil Count Arithmetic

For a 3-phase system with N_phases = 3 and N_coils_per_phase = k:
  Total coils = N_phases × k = 3k

Tabulation:
| k | Total coils | Configuration |
|---|-------------|---------------|
| 3 | 9           | 3×3 = 9       |
| 4 | 12          | 3×4 = 12      |
| 5 | 15          | 3×5 = 15      |
| 6 | 18          | 3×6 = 18      |

**Rotating field vector:**
The spatial distribution of N_coils equally spaced at angular positions θ_k = 2πk/N around the ring, each carrying a current phase-shifted by Δφ = 2π/N_phases from its neighbor in the same phase group:

  B_total(φ,t) = B_0 · cos(φ - ω·t)   [for ideal 3-phase symmetry]

This is a purely rotating magnetic field in the XY-plane — no pulsation, only rotation at angular frequency ω matching the electrical drive frequency. This smooth rotation is essential for stable, continuous spin of the central magnet without torque ripple.

**Pole pair geometry:**
For a 9-coil, 3-phase configuration (p = 3 pole pairs):
  Synchronous speed: ω_s = ω_electrical / p = ω_electrical / 3

For a 12-coil system (p = 4 pole pairs):
  ω_s = ω_electrical / 4

Fewer pole pairs = faster mechanical rotation for the same electrical frequency.
