### Ring Magnet Focal Point as Magnetic "Tabletop"

Source claim (Drew): The focal point of a ring magnet acts as an invisible "tabletop" on
which a spinning magnetic top can rise.

**Magnetic field structure of a ring (toroidal) magnet:**
A ring magnet magnetized axially produces a B-field with the following on-axis structure:

  B_z(z) = (μ₀ M / 2) × [ (L + z)/√(R² + (L+z)²) - z/√(R² + z²) ]

where R is the ring radius, L is the ring thickness, and z is axial distance from one face.
On the axis of symmetry, B_r = 0 by symmetry. The axial field has a maximum at the center
of the ring and decays above and below.

Off-axis, radial field components B_r exist and are responsible for the focusing/trapping
behavior. At the "focal point" on-axis, the field gradient ∂B_z/∂z changes sign — this is
a field saddle point. For a magnetic dipole m aligned with z, the force is:

  F_z = m · ∂B_z/∂z

At the saddle point above the ring center, the gradient ∂B_z/∂z < 0, providing an upward
restoring force on a downward-pointing dipole (normal levitation). However, Earnshaw's theorem
prohibits stable static levitation of a permanent magnet by static fields alone.

**Bypass of Earnshaw's theorem:** A spinning magnetic top (gyroscope) avoids Earnshaw's
theorem because gyroscopic stabilization provides the dynamic constraint needed to prevent
tipping instability. The spinning angular momentum L = Iω provides a large rotational
stiffness that couples to precessional motion rather than rolling over.

**XY-plane coupling to Z-axis stability:** The spin angular momentum (XY-plane rotation)
creates the gyroscopic effect that stabilizes the Z-axis position. This is the fundamental
XY→Z coupling claimed for this propulsion approach.

### Corbino Disc — Radial Current in Axial B Field → Azimuthal Force (Lorentz)

**Corbino disc geometry:**
- Disc conductor with central electrode and rim electrode
- Current density J flows radially: **J = J_r r̂**
- External axial magnetic field: **B = B_z ẑ**
- Lorentz force per unit volume: **f = J × B = J_r B_z (r̂ × ẑ) = -J_r B_z φ̂**

This produces a purely azimuthal (tangential) body force, inducing rotation of the disc.
There is no net axial (Z) force from the Corbino geometry alone — the rotation is entirely
in the XY-plane.

**Material: galinstan (Ga-In-Sn eutectic)**
- Liquid at room temperature (~−19°C melting point)
- High electrical conductivity (~3.46 × 10⁶ S/m, lower than pure metals but adequate)
- Allows free rotation without mechanical bearings — the liquid conductor can rotate
  about the Z-axis without solid-state friction
- Density ≈ 6440 kg/m³ — high enough for significant rotational inertia

**Operating current:** approximately 100 A DC
At 100 A and with galinstan conductivity, the current density and resulting J × B force
are sufficient to produce observable rotation at laboratory scale.

**Key claim:** "The Lorentz force allows 'spin' without requiring precisely-opposite
counter spin." This distinguishes the Corbino mechanism from mechanical action-reaction
systems: the azimuthal force is generated by the external static B field, which is the
implicit reaction partner (the magnetic field source/magnet structure bears the equal
and opposite torque). The disc experiences a net torque; the magnet structure experiences
the equal and opposite torque — but the "device" under study is the disc plus fluid only,
so no mechanical counter-rotation is needed within the test apparatus.

### Three-Phase AC as Rotating Magnetic Field Generator

Source claim (Drew): "Drawn in a field near [schematic version]." The Corbino disc and
its schematic crop-circle version illustrate 3-phase power driving rotation.

**Three-phase rotating magnetic field physics:**
Three sinusoidal currents at 120° phase offset flowing through three spatially separated
coils (offset by 120° around a circle) produce a net rotating magnetic field:

  B_x(t) = B₀ cos(ωt)
  B_y(t) = B₀ cos(ωt - 2π/3)  [from second coil, 120° offset]
  B_z(t) = B₀ cos(ωt - 4π/3)  [from third coil, 240° offset]

The superposition produces a field vector that rotates at angular frequency ω in the XY-plane
with constant magnitude B₀. This is the operating principle of AC induction motors.

three satellite circles at the Barbury Castle formation vertices are decoded as the

### Two Kinds of 3-Phase Magnetic Spin-Force: Chaotic vs. Laminar (Experimental)

Source claim (Drew): "IMPORTANT! PLEASE READ AND SHARE. There seem to be two kinds of
3-phase, magnetic spin-force (in general). Something like for 'chaotic' versus 'laminar' flow."

**Full experimental description of both magnetic regimes:**

LOW FREQUENCY REGIME (<20 Hz):
- Disc magnet spinning in 3-phase field moves strongly DOWNWARD (in one orientation) or
  UPWARD (in other orientation) on a bare aluminum or stainless steel rod
- No predictable rule found across 12+ different magnets for which direction occurs
- Force up to 1000 g (9.81 N) axial force on the pole/rod
- "Chaotic" analogy: different initial conditions (magnet orientation, geometry, magnetic
  moment magnitude) produce different outcomes (up vs. down) unpredictably
- The 3-phase rotating B field at low ω acts as a quasi-static driving force — the magnet
  tracks the rotating field and experiences the full gradient force ∇(m·B)

HIGH FREQUENCY REGIME (25-30 Hz):
- All magnets go upward (universal behavior, regardless of initial orientation)
- Magnet decouples from central pole: force on pole → 0
- Magnet hovers "like a GYROSCOPE"
- "Laminar" analogy: the behavior is predictable, universal, and orderly
- The 3-phase rotating B field at high ω creates a dynamically stabilized configuration
  where the gyroscopic L = Iω term dominates over direct field coupling
- Result: the interaction between the spinning magnet and the rotating B field is
  "laminar" in the sense of being smooth, stable, and directionally consistent (always upward)

**Aluminum and stainless steel rod — material significance:**
- Aluminum: non-ferromagnetic, high electrical conductivity (~3.77 × 10⁷ S/m)
  → Eddy currents induced in Al rod by spinning magnet's B field
  → Lenz's law: eddy current creates opposing force — effectively a magnetic brake
  → At low ω: eddy current force is small (proportional to ω²σ)
  → At high ω: eddy current force becomes significant; may play role in "decoupling"

- Stainless steel (austenitic): non-ferromagnetic (μ_r ≈ 1.003), lower conductivity
  (~1.4 × 10⁶ S/m) — reduced eddy current effect vs. aluminum
  → Both materials show same qualitative behavior (two regimes) despite different conductivities

This eliminates eddy currents as the sole explanation for the regime transition (if it were
purely eddy currents, aluminum would show much stronger effect than stainless, and the
transition frequency would differ significantly).

**The gyroscopic magnetic field — never seen before:**
"Such a 'gyroscopic' motion has never been seen before using iron-free, 3-phase, copper
electromagnets to create spin. We need a 'pole' for stability."

Iron-free specification: conventional electromagnetic actuators use iron cores to concentrate
flux (iron has μ_r ~ 1000-10000). Iron-free means:
  B_field from copper coils only: B = μ₀ n I (no iron permeability enhancement)
  → Weaker field but no ferromagnetic saturation or hysteresis losses
  → Field topology is purely determined by coil geometry, not distorted by iron

This is significant because ferromagnetic components would complicate the field topology
(paramagnetism of iron vs. the tested magnet creates additional coupling). Iron-free ensures
the observed behavior is due to the fundamental interaction between the spinning permanent
magnet and the pure copper-coil rotating field.

**Connection to Eric Laithwaite:**
Drew references Eric Laithwaite's 1970s work: "This may be 'related' to what Eric Laithwaite
saw in the 1970s, when he made a big mechanical spinning gyroscope, but his conclusions
likewise seem vague, and not easily amenable to further direct, small-scale experimentation."

Laithwaite (Imperial College London) demonstrated large gyroscopes (~10 kg) that appeared to

resistance to torque in the wrong direction). Drew suggests Laithwaite may have observed a
real phenomenon but lacked the framework to describe it clearly.

### Anomalous Counter-Torque via Electromagnetic Coupling (Video Observation)

Source claim (video): Wires rotate one direction; disc rotates the opposite direction.
The wires are "not enough mass to start the disc rotating" — yet the disc does rotate.

**Standard Lorentz torque analysis:**
In a current-carrying loop of wire in a magnetic field, the torque is:
  τ = m × B
where m is the magnetic moment of the current loop. The wire loop and disc are mechanically
coupled through the shared magnetic field, not through direct mechanical linkage.

If the wire carries current I in a spatially distributed path and is in field B, the torque
on the wire is:
  τ_wire = ∮ (r × (I dl × B))

The equal and opposite torque is borne by the source of B (the permanent magnet or
electromagnet producing the field). The disc, if it is a conducting disc in the same
field, will experience an induced-current torque (eddy current torque) from Faraday
induction as the wire-carried field rotates relative to it.

**Anomaly stated:** The mass of the wire is insufficient to mechanically drive the disc via
any contact force or inertia transfer. This implies the torque transfer path is entirely
electromagnetic — through the field — not mechanical. The observation that the torques
are described as "not equal and opposite" suggests the system is not fully conservative
in the mechanical sense and may be coupling to an external field (Earth's magnetic field,
or the support structure's inductance).
