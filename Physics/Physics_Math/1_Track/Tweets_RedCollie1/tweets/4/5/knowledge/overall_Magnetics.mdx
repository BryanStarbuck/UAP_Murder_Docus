### Ring Permanent Magnet in a Three-Phase Rotating Field: Polarity-Dependent Axial Force

**Magnet geometry:**
- Ring permanent magnet: 100 mm outer diameter, 13 mm central hole, mounted on 8 mm stainless steel rod
- South (blue) pole orientation: pole axis pointing upward
- North (red) pole orientation: pole axis pointing upward

**Field source:**
- Three-phase AC driving three coil assemblies positioned at 120-degree intervals in the XY-plane
- The three-phase current sequence U-V-W creates a rotating magnetic field in the XY-plane; the field vector rotates continuously at the driving frequency

**Polarity selectivity of the upward force:**
- Blue South pole up: strong, repeatable upward Z-axis displacement. Magnet rises from base to the center/top of the coil region. This behavior is consistent under both clockwise and anti-clockwise rotation of the driving field — the force is polarity-sensitive but spin-direction-insensitive.
- Red North pole up: magnet spins and rises slightly but does not produce full axial displacement. Upward force is strongly suppressed for North-up orientation.

**Inversion of spin direction between top and bottom subsystems:**
"In every case, the upward pole spin-motion for blue-up permanent magnets is always opposite to spin-motion in the magnet below." The rising magnet counter-rotates relative to the driving field or the base magnetic assembly. This constitutes a spontaneous angular momentum counter-rotation arrangement — consistent with the Norman Paulson UAP witness account of two magnetic discs rotating in opposite directions.

**Significance for field topology:**
The axial (Z-direction) force on the magnet depends on the relative orientation of the magnet's magnetic dipole moment **m** with the topology of the rotating field's gradient. For a dipole in a non-uniform field **B**, the force is **F** = ∇(**m** · **B**). The polarity selectivity (South-up gives lift, North-up does not) indicates that the symmetry of the rotating field gradient favors one dipole orientation over the other for axial translation. The rotating field is not purely transverse; it generates an axial gradient component that is sign-dependent on the orientation of the test magnet's dipole.

### Six-Coil Three-Phase Spin / Counter-Spin Device: Complete Magnetic Architecture

**Coil assembly:**
- Six wire coils, each wound from approximately 90 mm × 0.5 mm PEA-insulated copper wire, arranged in a ring
- Wired in series pairs: U-U, V-V, W-W (UVW UVW three-phase)
- Each series pair has resistance 25 Ω + 25 Ω = 50 Ω
- Power supply: 240 V, three-phase, variable frequency; ATO (California) variable frequency drive
- At 240 V across 50 Ω series pairs, current ≈ 240/50 = 4.8 A per circuit
- Field rotation frequency variable: 6 Hz demonstrated at low power (visible spin), 15–17 Hz demonstrated at higher speeds

**Central rotating element:**
- Single ring magnet, 70 mm diameter, 14 mm central hole, mounted on 8 mm stainless steel rod
- Spins clockwise with the rotating field (co-rotation)

**Outer counter-rotating element:**
- Eight disc magnets, each 30 mm diameter, arranged on a 90 mm bore zirconia non-magnetic bearing
- Alternating polarity: N-S, N-S, N-S, N-S (4 pairs of alternating poles around the circumference)
- Bearing lubricated with Teflon (PTFE) spray for low-friction free spin
- Spins anti-clockwise against the rotating field (counter-rotation)

**Field coupling mechanism:**
The six coils produce a rotating B-field vector in the XY-plane, rotating clockwise at the drive frequency. The central single magnet is dragged clockwise by this field (synchronous or near-synchronous rotation). The outer ring of eight alternating-polarity magnets, being arranged in an NSNS pattern with period approximately equal to the field wavelength at the ring circumference, experiences a force that results in counter-rotation. The anti-clockwise spin of the outer ring relative to a clockwise field arises from the induced eddy-current / induced-field reaction, or from the spatial phase mismatch between the alternating-pole array and the rotating drive field — analogous to asynchronous motor operation where the rotor slip reverses effective driving direction.

**XY-plane magnetic dynamics:**
- The rotating field from the six coils establishes a net angular momentum vector along +Z (clockwise field = positive Z spin in right-hand convention)
- Inner magnet carries +Z angular momentum (co-rotating)
- Outer ring carries −Z angular momentum (counter-rotating)
- Net angular momentum: L_total = L_inner + L_outer; the algebraic sum is partially or fully cancelled, depending on moment of inertia ratio and rotation rates

**Proposed Z-axis consequence:**
Counter-rotating magnetic discs are hypothesized to decouple or modify the action-reaction pair that would normally arise from a single rotating magnetic system. Drew states: "we might expect two, counter-rotating, magnetic discs to have strong and novel effects on Newton's Third Law of action-reaction." The magnetic basis is that when two oppositely-spinning magnetized discs occupy the same axial position, the net rotating magnetic moment ≈ 0, but the gradient of the magnetic energy density in the Z-direction may be non-zero due to the geometric asymmetry of the configuration, potentially producing a net axial force on the center-of-mass of the system without equal and opposite reaction to an external medium.

### Gyroscopic Behavior of Fast-Spinning Magnets

The referenced tweet states: "a fast-spinning magnet acts like a gyroscope, and thus is [able to maintain its orientation]." This is significant for the magnetic architecture: a gyroscopically stabilized spinning permanent magnet maintains its dipole orientation in space regardless of external perturbations. The gyroscopic stiffness (resistance to precession) scales as L = I·ω, where I is the moment of inertia and ω is the angular velocity. For a disc magnet of mass m and radius r, I = mr²/2. At high spin rates the gyroscopic stiffness dominates over gravitational torque, allowing the magnet to maintain its pole orientation as it rises. This is distinct from the "wobbling" behavior observed at low spin rates (where precession is significant), which Drew also notes as part of the observed phenomenology ("the spinning and wobbling (rising) magnet").
