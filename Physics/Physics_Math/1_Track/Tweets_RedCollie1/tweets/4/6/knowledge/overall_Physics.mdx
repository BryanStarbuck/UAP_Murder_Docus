### XY-Plane Dynamics (Stage 1)

The physical scenario is a conducting fluid of conductivity σ placed in a static axial magnetic field **B** = B_z ẑ, with an applied electric potential difference ΔV across the fluid.

The current density in the fluid:

**J** = σ(**E** + **v** × **B**)

where **E** = −∇V is the electric field from the applied DC voltage and **v** is the fluid velocity. Initially, **v** = 0, so:

**J**₀ = σ**E** = σ(−∇V)

For a radial electrode configuration, **J**₀ ≈ J_r r̂ (radially directed current).

The initial Lorentz body force:

**f** = **J**₀ × **B** = J_r B_z (r̂ × ẑ) = −J_r B_z φ̂

This azimuthal body force accelerates the fluid in the φ̂ direction. As the fluid gains azimuthal velocity v_φ φ̂, a back-EMF appears: **v** × **B** = v_φ φ̂ × B_z ẑ = v_φ B_z (φ̂ × ẑ) = v_φ B_z r̂, which modifies the radial current density. At steady-state (when viscous drag equals driving force), the fluid reaches a terminal azimuthal angular velocity Ω_fluid.

Steady-state for viscous fluid (simplified, cylindrical geometry):

Ω_fluid ≈ (J_r B_z R²) / (2η)

where R is the characteristic radius of the fluid volume and η is the dynamic viscosity. For low-viscosity liquid metals (galinstan: η ≈ 2.0 × 10⁻³ Pa·s), high Ω_fluid values are achievable even at modest B_z and J_r.

Experimental parameters (tabletop): ΔV = 50 V, B_z from 80 mm neodymium magnet (surface field ≈ 0.3–0.5 T), fluid = salt water (σ ≈ 5–10 S/m). Observed result: clear azimuthal rotation demonstrated visually with food dye.

Proposed operational parameters: ΔV = 40,000–50,000 V, B_z from large electromagnet (potentially > 1 T), fluid = galinstan (σ ≈ 3.46 × 10⁶ S/m).

B_x(t) = (2/3)B₀[cos(ωt) - (1/2)cos(ωt - 2π/3) - (1/2)cos(ωt - 4π/3)]
B_y(t) = (2/3)B₀[-(√3/2)cos(ωt - 2π/3) + (√3/2)cos(ωt - 4π/3)]

Simplifying: **B**_rot(t) = B₀[cos(ωt) x̂ + sin(ωt) ŷ] — a field of constant magnitude B₀ rotating at angular frequency ω in the XY-plane.

### Standard Farnsworth Fusor (Slide 1 baseline)

Electrostatic confinement: A spherical or cylindrical anode (grounded or at positive HV) surrounds a negatively biased cathode grid. Ions are accelerated toward the cathode through a potential difference of −V_cathode (typically −40 to −100 kV).

For deuterium: m_D = 3.344 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, q = +e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

At 40 kV: KE = 4 × 10⁴ eV = 40 keV >> k_B T_room ≈ 0.025 eV

Fusion cross-section σ_D-D at 40 keV center-of-mass energy ≈ 10⁻²⁹ m² (well below practical ignition threshold). The standard fusor operates far below breakeven due to isotropic ion scattering and direct ion losses to the cathode grid.

### Drew's Modified Fusor (Slide 2: +DC Electromagnet)

Addition of a DC electromagnet with field B_DC along the symmetry axis. The axial B-field causes ions to follow helical trajectories:

r_L = mv⊥ / (|q|B_DC)  [Larmor radius]
ω_c = |q|B_DC / m  [cyclotron angular frequency]

For argon ions (Ar⁺, m = 6.634 × 10⁻²⁶ kg) at 40 keV radial energy and B_DC = 0.1 T:
v⊥ ≈ (2 × 40,000 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 6.634 × 10⁻²⁶)^(1/2) ≈ 4.9 × 10⁵ m/s
r_L ≈ (6.634 × 10⁻²⁶ × 4.9 × 10⁵) / (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.1) ≈ 0.020 m = 20 mm

The DC B-field confines ions to helical spirals, reducing their radial transit to the cathode grid and increasing the average path length before grid collision — improving confinement slightly. "This is just one Lorentz force" (Drew's description).

B_AC(t) = B₀ cos(2π × 50 × t)

Ions undergoing clockwise Larmor orbits (during positive half-cycle) switch to counter-clockwise orbits (during negative half-cycle) every 0.01 seconds (at 50 Hz). This creates two populations of ions: CW-orbiting and CCW-orbiting, meeting at the center at crossing times.

The head-on collision condition: For two ions approaching the center from diametrically opposite points in their Larmor orbits, the relative velocity at the crossing point is v_relative ≈ 2v_radial. "Two Lorentz forces, which alternate in time clockwise versus anti-clockwise, 50 times per second." — This is the proposed physical mechanism for the hot central region.

## Section: 1912108172971790596 (referenced) — "Upward Pole" Force: Spinning Magnet in 3-Phase Field

Physical claim: A 100 mm permanent magnet with axial rod, spinning in a 3-phase magnetic field, exhibits a net upward (Z-axis) force exclusively when its South pole faces upward.

Physical interpretation: The force on a magnetic dipole **m** in an external field **B** is:

**F** = ∇(**m** · **B**)

In a spatially uniform rotating field, this gradient is zero and no net force results. However, in a 3-phase winding with finite spatial extent, the field has both a rotating component (constant-amplitude rotating vector) and higher-order multipole components that have spatial gradients. The interaction of the spinning magnet's magnetic moment with these gradient terms can produce a net time-averaged force.

For a magnet spinning at angular frequency ω_spin in a field rotating at angular frequency ω_field:
- If ω_spin = ω_field (synchronous): The magnet "locks in" to the rotating field. The relative angle between **m** and **B**_rot is constant. The force depends on the spatial gradient of |B| at the magnet's location.
- If ω_spin ≠ ω_field (asynchronous): Time-averaging of the force over one beat cycle (2π/(ω_field − ω_spin)) determines the net force.

The orientation-dependence (South pole up only): This implies the sign of the Z-axis force depends on the relative handedness of the magnet's spin direction (set by the initial Lorentz drive, which in turn depends on B_z polarity) and the handedness of the rotating field (set by the phase sequence of the 3-phase supply). Only specific combinations of these two handedness parameters produce a net upward (rather than downward or zero) Z-axis force.

This is an experimental result, not a derived prediction — the orientation-dependence is claimed as observed in the laboratory.

The "falling motion of a gyroscope" references standard gyroscopic precession under gravitational torque:

τ = **r** × M**g** (applied torque on spinning gyroscope supported at pivot)

This requires τ_EM = Mgr everywhere along the gyroscope's orbit — a dynamic control requirement, not a passive property.

## Section: 1980904332179714156.yaml — Fast-Spinning Magnet as Gyroscope: Spin Stabilization

"A fast-spinning magnet acts like a gyroscope, and thus is [stabilized]" — this references the gyroscopic rigidity of a spinning body:

The angular momentum of a spinning magnet: **L** = Iω n̂, where I is the moment of inertia about the spin axis, ω is the angular velocity, and n̂ is the unit vector along the spin axis.

Gyroscopic rigidity: The spinning body resists changes to the orientation of n̂. The effective rigidity (resistance to angular deflection) scales as |L| = Iω. A fast-spinning magnet (high ω) thus maintains its spin axis orientation more stably under external perturbations.

For a toroidal/donut-shaped neodymium magnet with an axial rod: the rod defines the spin axis mechanically, and the gyroscopic rigidity of the spinning assembly stabilizes n̂ against wobble. This is the mechanical reason the rod is "needed for spin stability" (tweet 1920987068525658515).

The central hole and rod geometry: The toroid's moment of inertia about its symmetry axis (the rod/hole axis):

I_axial = (1/2) M(R₁² + R₂²)

where R₁ is the inner radius (hole radius = 13 mm / 2 = 6.5 mm) and R₂ is the outer radius (100 mm / 2 = 50 mm). This gives a high moment of inertia for the given mass, maximizing gyroscopic rigidity for a given angular velocity ω.

These three disciplines are the core physics frameworks underlying Drew's experimental work:

1. **Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD)**: The coupling of fluid mechanics and electromagnetism in conducting fluids. Governing equations: Navier-Stokes + Maxwell's equations + Ohm's law for moving conductors. The MHD body force **f** = **J** × **B** drives azimuthal fluid rotation in Drew's tabletop experiments.

2. **Electrohydrodynamics (EHD)**: The coupling of electric fields and fluid mechanics, particularly for dielectric or weakly conducting fluids. At the high voltages Drew proposes (40–50 kV), EHD effects (electrostriction, ion drag, space-charge forces) become significant even in nominally non-conducting fluids.

3. **Vortex modeling**: The mathematical description of rotating fluid elements. In the context of Drew's experiments, the azimuthally rotating fluid driven by Lorentz forces forms a toroidal vortex structure. The interaction of this toroidal vortex with the 3-phase magnetic field is the proposed source of Z-axis lift. Vortex dynamics is governed by the vorticity transport equation:

∂ω/∂t + (v·∇)ω = (ω·∇)v + ν∇²ω + (1/ρ)∇ρ × ∇P + (1/ρ)∇ × (J × B)

where the last term is the MHD source of vorticity from Lorentz forces.
