### Static Magnetic Focal-Point Stability: Violation of Earnshaw's Theorem Conditions

Standard result: Earnshaw's theorem states that no static configuration of charges (or magnetic
monopoles) can produce a stable equilibrium of a test charge in free space. For magnetic dipoles,
the theorem generalizes: a permanent magnetic dipole cannot be held in stable equilibrium by static
magnetic fields alone in 3D space (Braunbek 1939 corollary). This is the basis for mainstream
rejection of passive magnetic levitation.

Experimental challenge (Drew / video): A floater magnet of diameter d ≤ d_hole (where d_hole is
the inner diameter of the ring magnet) simultaneously experiences:
- Axial (Z-axis) repulsion: field lines exit the ring's pole face axially, repelling a same-polarity
  floater upward
- Radial (XY-plane) inward attraction: field lines at the inner edge of the ring curve radially
  inward, creating an inward force that confines the floater near the ring axis

The combined Z-repulsion + XY-confinement constitutes an axisymmetric potential well — a toroidal
trap geometry. Earnshaw's theorem is not violated because the floater is not in full 3D equilibrium;
the XY confinement is a saddle, not a minimum, in 3D. However, in 2D (XY-plane) the arrangement
is effectively stable for small displacements, and in Z, repulsion counteracts gravity. The system
may be marginally stable (saddle-point levitation) rather than unconditionally stable.

Stability criterion:
d_floater ≤ d_hole (necessary condition from experiment)
This implies the floater's dipole field must be contained within the hole's radial extent so that
the competing inward component of the ring's field dominates over the outward component at the
floater's edge.

Stated experimental ranges:
- 145 mm ferrite ring, 80 mm hole: stable for floaters 40–80 mm; unstable for 100 mm
- 100 mm NdFeB ring, 60 mm hole: stable for floaters 40–60 mm; unstable for 70–80 mm

### Gyroscopic Precession: Euler Equations and Laithwaite Anomaly (1974)

Classical gyroscope equations of motion (Euler):

dL/dt = τ

where L = I ω (angular momentum) and τ is applied torque. For a gyroscope spinning about ẑ
with L = L ẑ, and torque τ applied along x̂:

τ x̂ = dL/dt → precession about ŷ

Precession rate: Ω_p = τ / (I ω) = τ / L

This is rigorously classical. Laithwaite's demonstration (Imperial College / Royal Institution,
1974) showed a heavy gyroscope precessing under gravity rather than falling — counterintuitive
but fully within classical mechanics.

Laithwaite's claimed anomaly (experimental, disputed):
"Our weight rising means an increase in potential energy. Where did the energy come from?"

If a suspended weight attached to a precessing gyroscope rises as the gyroscope precesses, the
potential energy gain ΔPE = mgh must be sourced from somewhere. In classical mechanics, it comes
from:
1. The kinetic energy of precession: T_precession = ½ I_p Ω_p² where I_p is moment of inertia
   about the precession axis
2. The kinetic energy of spin: T_spin = ½ I ω²

Laithwaite's interpretation was that the input work from the "finger push" was anomalously small
compared to ΔPE. The mainstream rebuttal is that his energy accounting was incomplete — the
precession kinetic energy term was neglected.

Drew treats this as unresolved and physically interesting for UAP propulsion context.
