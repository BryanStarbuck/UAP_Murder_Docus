### Physical Content

The observation of "distortion" when spinning parallel to gravity (vs. perpendicular) implies the experimenter is looking for a gravitomagnetic coupling — where the rotation of a magnetic body along the gravity axis modifies the local effective gravity.

In general relativity, the Lense-Thirring gravitomagnetic field from a spinning mass M with angular momentum J is:
B_g = (G/c^2) * [3(J*r_hat)r_hat - J] / r^3

However, if the claim is that spinning magnetic fields couple to gravity through a non-GR mechanism, this is the experimental test: rotating a magnet aligned with g and looking for deviations from expected eddy-current or gyroscopic behavior.

The switching of inner rotation direction between "inside" and "outside" the apparatus suggests a spatially varying field interaction — possibly the stator field switches which harmonic dominates at different radii, changing the effective coupling.

## 7. Anti-Gravity vs. Gravity — Definitional Issue

The term "anti-gravity" is used across multiple tweets (ids 1933291638690099659, 1966040356119797872) without specifying the mechanism. In the context of this experimental program, "anti-gravity" appears to mean:

(a) A net upward force (Z-positive) produced by electromagnetic means, without aerodynamic lift surfaces (no wings, no air jets)
(b) Not necessarily a modification of the gravitational field itself (g does not change)
(c) More precisely: an electromagnetic-inertial force that opposes gravity locally, allowing levitation or flight

This distinguishes the claim from:
- Gravitational shielding (modifying G or g locally — not claimed)
- Alcubierre warp drive (metric engineering — not claimed in this context)
- Higgs field manipulation (not claimed)

The claim is purely mechanical/electromagnetic: spinning magnetic field + conducting medium → Z-axis force → flight.

Whether this force can scale to craft-relevant magnitudes is not addressed in the available material. The laboratory demonstrations are described as small-scale ("reverse-engineered, small-scale device").
