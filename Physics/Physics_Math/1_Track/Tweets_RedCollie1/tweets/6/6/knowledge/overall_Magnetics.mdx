### Magnetic Dipole Field Visualization — Bar Magnet / Solenoid

The image depicts the classical magnetic dipole field B(r) in three-dimensional space for a cylindrical bar magnet or solenoid:

    B(r) = (μ₀/4π) · [3(m·r̂)r̂ - m] / r³

where:
- μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A (permeability of free space)
- m = magnetic dipole moment vector (aligned with solenoid axis)
- r = position vector from dipole center
- r̂ = unit radial vector

**Field structure observed:**
- North pole (right end): field lines emerge radially outward (+r direction near pole)
- South pole (left end): field lines return radially inward
- Far field: field lines form closed loops following the dipole topology
- Equatorial plane: B vectors point parallel to dipole axis (along ẑ if axis is horizontal)
- Axial field (on-axis): B_axial = (μ₀/4π) · (2m/r³)
- Equatorial field: B_equatorial = -(μ₀/4π) · (m/r³)

**Vector field arrows (yellow/gold):**
Sampled B-vectors at discrete points. Arrow magnitude ∝ |B(r)|, decreasing as r⁻³ with distance. Near poles: maximum density. Far from magnet: sparse.

**Blue curved arrow on cylinder:**
Indicates the direction of current circulation producing the magnetic moment, consistent with the right-hand rule: current loops in the plane perpendicular to ẑ, flowing in the direction such that the thumb (ẑ) points toward the north pole.

**Context — UAP Propulsion Research:**
This dipole field visualization is directly relevant to rotating magnetic field configurations used in UAP propulsion experiments. The solenoid (with wound coils) is the laboratory analogue of the "wire coil" elements in Drew's experimental setups. Understanding the B-field topology of individual coils is prerequisite to analyzing the superposed rotating field produced by phased multi-coil arrays.

### Six-Coil Three-Phase Rotating Magnetic Field — Laboratory Implementation

**Physical setup:**

**Three-phase field physics:**
For 3-phase balanced sinusoidal currents at phases 0°, 120°, 240° (UVW):
- I_U(t) = I₀ cos(ωt)
- I_V(t) = I₀ cos(ωt - 2π/3)
- I_W(t) = I₀ cos(ωt - 4π/3)

Each coil produces a pulsating B-field. Superposition of 3 coils at 120° spatial intervals:

    B_total(t) = B₀ · (3/2) · [cos(ωt) x̂ + sin(ωt) ŷ]

This is a uniformly rotating field of constant magnitude (3/2)B₀ rotating at angular frequency ω in the XY-plane. The factor 3/2 is the amplitude enhancement from superposition.

**Six-coil configuration (two sets UVW):**

- Set 1 (CW sequence): produces B rotating clockwise in XY-plane
- Set 2 (CCW sequence): produces B rotating counter-clockwise

This results in a standing-wave or counter-rotating superposed field depending on relative phases. If the two sets are exactly counter-phased:
    B_total = B_CW + B_CCW → linear (non-rotating) oscillating field
If staggered:

**Observed results:**

- Six upper coils only: outer flywheel spins CCW, inner magnet spins CW
- Net angular momentum L_total = L_flywheel(CCW) + L_magnet(CW) ≈ 0

**Frequency scaling:**

### Tesla Unipolar Motor — Opposite-of-Textbook Magnetic Principle

Tesla's 1901 Unipolar Motor (homopolar motor) operates on a magnetic principle Drew describes as "the opposite of what they teach in schools":

Standard AC induction motor: alternating B-field induces eddy currents → force on conductor (Lenz's law based)
Unipolar motor: **static B-field** + rotating conductor → continuous DC torque from motional EMF:

    EMF = ∫(v × B) · dl

For a disk of radius r rotating at ω in axial field B_z:
    EMF = (1/2) · B_z · ω · r²

The current flows radially outward (or inward) under this EMF, and the radial current in the axial B-field produces a tangential (φ̂) force by F = IL × B:
    F_φ = I_r · L · B_z

This is a pure DC torque with no commutation, no torque ripple, and no back-EMF reversal. The "opposite of textbook": textbook motors use AC rotating fields; the unipolar uses static field + rotating conductor.
