## A. Gyroscopic Physics and Precession

### Standard Gyroscope Under Three-Phase Drive
A spinning permanent magnet on an axle under three-phase AC drive behaves initially as a
gyroscope. The torque-free precession equation for a symmetric top is:

  dL/dt = τ = r × F

For a gyroscope with angular momentum L = I × ω (moment of inertia I, angular velocity ω),
precession under applied torque τ gives:

  Ω_precession = τ / (I × ω_spin)

where Ω_precession is the precession angular velocity, τ = |r × mg| = m × g × r × sin(θ),
and θ is the tilt angle from vertical.

The author observes that at low rpm the magnet behaves like a normal gyroscope — it spins and
wobbles (precesses). At higher rpm, instead of simply precessing, the magnet rises along the
vertical axis. This rise is claimed to be non-Newtonian.
Source: MDX 13_1945035075034189824_video; YAML 1945035257058656324.

### Why a Three-Axis Gyroscope Cannot Be Used
A three-axis gyroscope (gimbal mount) has universal joint freedom in all rotational degrees of
freedom. The author notes that the "essential motion of the spinning and wobbling won't take
place because of the connections" — the gimbal constraints prevent the specific precession
mode required for UFO levitation. An "axial ball joint" is proposed instead, which constrains
the magnet's central position while permitting the wobble/precession.

## B. Homopolar Generator / Faraday Disc Physics

### Standard Faraday Disc (Homopolar Generator)
A conducting disc of inner radius r₁ and outer radius r₂ spinning with angular velocity ω
in a uniform axial magnetic field B generates a radial EMF:

  EMF = (1/2) × B × ω × (r₂² - r₁²)

Current flows radially from the axle (hub) to the rim (or vice versa, depending on field direction
and rotation sense). The current is extracted via sliding contacts at the hub and rim. This is
the Faraday unipolar induction effect, which produces DC (not AC) output because the flux
linkage increases monotonically with rotation angle (no commutation required).

## D. Rotating Magnetic Field and Spin Induction (Beyond Classical Induction)

### Classical Faraday Induction (Standard EM)
A time-varying magnetic flux Φ through a conducting loop induces EMF:
  EMF = -dΦ/dt (Faraday's law)

For a rotating field B(t) = B₀ × [cos(ωt) x̂ + sin(ωt) ŷ], the flux through a conducting disc
lying in the XY-plane is:
  Φ = ∫ B · dA = 0 (since B is in XY plane and dA is along Z for a horizontal disc)

Therefore a horizontal rotating field produces zero Faraday induction in a horizontal conducting
disc — no torque by classical induction.

### Author's Claim: Not Induction
The author explicitly states (YAML 1963247821026738254):
"Not 'induction'. And it doesn't matter whether the North or South pole faces a horizontal,
rotating magnetic field."

This is correct in the following sense: for a permanent magnet with its dipole moment aligned
vertically (along Z) placed in a horizontally-rotating field, classical EM predicts a torque:
  τ = m × B = m_z ẑ × [B₀(cos(ωt) x̂ + sin(ωt) ŷ)] = m_z B₀ [-sin(ωt) x̂ + cos(ωt) ŷ]

This torque would tend to tilt the magnet toward the XY-plane, not spin it around the Z-axis.
Yet the author observes Z-axis spin. He attributes this to the helical field topology rather
than the classical rotating dipole field.

A helical magnetic field B = B_r r̂ + B_φ φ̂ + B_z ẑ in cylindrical coordinates (r, φ, z),
with azimuthal and radial components winding along the Z-axis, could in principle exert
an axial torque on the spinning magnet's magnetic moment through:
  τ_z = m_r × B_φ - m_φ × B_r

where m_r and m_φ are radial and azimuthal components of the magnetic moment induced or
permanent. This is distinct from classical induction and is the mechanism the author claims.

## E. Three-Phase AC Motor Physics (Standard Baseline)

Standard three-phase induction motor: rotating magnetic field at supply frequency f_s (e.g., 50
or 60 Hz) drives rotor at slip frequency:
  f_rotor = f_s × (1 - s)
where s is the slip (0 < s < 1 for induction, s = 0 for synchronous).

The torque-speed characteristic of a three-phase motor:
  T = (3 × V_phase² × R_rotor/s) / (ω_s × [(R_stator + R_rotor/s)² + (X_stator + X_rotor)²])

At low slip, T ≈ (3 × V_phase² × s) / (ω_s × R_rotor) — torque proportional to slip.

The author's device uses permanent magnets rather than a conducting rotor, so it operates in
synchronous mode (s = 0), with the magnet locked to the rotating field frequency.
Source: MDX 13_1945035075034189824_video (description of three-phase power and rise behavior).

Physical analysis of why three-axis gyroscope fails:

the spinning element in a way that prevents the specific nutation/wobble mode needed. Specifically:

to a fixed point (no translation). The rise requires translational freedom along Z while
simultaneously permitting the nutation wobble. The axial ball joint satisfies this: it acts as
a spherical constraint on the spin axis orientation (allowing wobble) while permitting translation
along the primary axis (allowing rise).
