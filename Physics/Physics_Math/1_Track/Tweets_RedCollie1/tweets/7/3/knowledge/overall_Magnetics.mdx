## 1. Three-Phase Rotating Magnetic Field (r.m.f.) — Axial Flux Configuration

Source: YAML 2017877032907247916 (note_tweet), YAML 2021005921309385050 (note_tweet),
        YAML 1978591957686661487 (note_tweet), MDX 13_2017872609355116544_video.mdx,
        MDX 13_2007699282548404229_video.mdx

### Configuration Description

* Three copper wire coils arranged symmetrically (120° apart) around a central axis.
* Driven by 3-phase AC power — three sinusoidal currents phase-shifted by 120° relative to
  each other: I_A(t) = I_0 sin(ωt), I_B(t) = I_0 sin(ωt + 2π/3), I_C(t) = I_0 sin(ωt + 4π/3).
* The superposition of the three fields produces a resultant magnetic field vector that rotates
  at angular frequency ω in the plane perpendicular to the coil axis — this is the rotating
  magnetic field (r.m.f.) or rotating magnetic flux density B_rot(t).
* The flux geometry is "axial flux" — the rotating field vector lies in the XY-plane (horizontal
  plane), and the axis of rotation is the Z-axis (vertical). This is the relevant component
  for producing upward spin of a ring magnet on a central vertical rod.

### Distinction from DC and Single-Phase

* DC field: static B-field. No rotation, no induced spin. A ring magnet on a rod with DC
  excitation would be attracted or repelled but would not spin or rise.
* Single-phase AC: oscillating (not rotating) field — B(t) = B_0 sin(ωt) ẑ or similar.
  Produces vibration but not net rotation of a suspended magnet.
* 3-phase only: produces true spatially rotating field. This is the condition stated as
  necessary for the observed spin+rise behavior. YAML 2017877032907247916: "we need to use
  copper electromagnets, rather than permanent magnets, to create a rotating magnetic field
  with 3-phase AC power."

### Observed Behavior with 3-Phase Axial-Flux r.m.f.

* YAML 2017877032907247916: "with copper electromagnets, and a 3-phase magnetic field (of
  axial flux), even large permanent magnets (of 70 mm diameter or more) will rise up strongly
  when they are spinning, in both N-up and S-up orientations."
* The N-up / S-up orientation-independence is the critical control result ruling out dipole-
  specific mechanisms.

## 3. XY-Plane and Z-Axis Magnetic Dynamics

Source: YAML 2017877032907247916 (note_tweet), YAML 2021005921309385050 (note_tweet),
        YAML 1978591957686661487 (note_tweet)

### XY-Plane (Horizontal Plane) Dynamics

* The 3-phase rotating magnetic field rotates in the XY-plane (horizontal plane).
* The outer counter-spinning flywheel is to be positioned "in the same approximate two-
  dimensional plane (or same height)" as the inner spinning magnet — i.e., the same XY-plane.
* YAML 2017877032907247916: "trying to spin another magnetic flywheel in an opposite
  direction to the big inner magnet, but further away from the center, in the same
  approximate two-dimensional plane (or same height)."
* The rationale: if both spin-up reaction and spin-down reaction are in the same XY-plane
  but at different radii from center, the reaction forces can potentially cancel in the Z
  direction while the gyroscopic redirections combine.

### Z-Axis (Vertical) Dynamics — Upward Force

* The ring magnet rises along the Z-axis (upward, along the rod) when the XY-plane r.m.f.
  is applied.
* Z-axis rise is orientation-independent (N-up and S-up both rise with 8 mm bore / 5 mm rod).
* The downward Newton's 3rd Law reaction force also acts along Z.
* Counter-spin strategy: apply an equal and opposite angular momentum in the XY-plane at
  larger radius; gyroscopic precession then redirects the Z-axis reaction force into the
  XY-plane (lateral), leaving the net Z-axis force unbalanced upward.

### The Lorentz Homopolar Force — Counter-Spin Mechanism

Source: YAML 1978591957686661487 (note_tweet)

* The downward force opposing the spinning magnet's upward rise is identified as the
  "Lorentz homopolar force."
* Homopolar (Faraday disk) geometry: a conducting disc rotating in an axial magnetic field
  generates a radial EMF given by V = (1/2)ωBR², where ω is the angular velocity, B is the
  axial field, R is the disc radius. The associated radial current in a closed circuit
  produces an axial Lorentz force (F = IL × B) that opposes the rotation — i.e., a braking
  torque and an axial force component.
* In Drew's model: the spinning ring magnet in the axial 3-phase field creates a homopolar
  effect, and the resulting Lorentz force has a downward Z-component that equals the
  upward levitation force (Newton's 3rd Law).
