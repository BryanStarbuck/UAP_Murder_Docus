## 1. Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF) — Core Phenomenon

### 1a. Physical Description

A rotating magnetic field (RMF) is produced when a set of spatially distributed wire coils
(stator windings) are driven by polyphase AC current with defined phase offsets. For a
3-phase system with coils at 120° angular separation, currents I_1 = I_0 sin(ωt),
I_2 = I_0 sin(ωt - 2π/3), I_3 = I_0 sin(ωt + 2π/3) produce a resultant magnetic field
vector B(t) that rotates in the XY-plane at angular frequency ω. The field magnitude
remains approximately constant while the direction rotates — this is the defining property
of the RMF.

Drew describes this as "all those wavy lines like a three-phase or five-phase field"
(video transcript 13_1927585094572630016_video.mdx).

### 1b. Extension to 5-Phase and 6-Phase AC

For 5-phase AC: 5 coils at 72° angular separation, with phase offsets 2πk/5 for k = 0..4,
produce a 5-phase RMF. Drew associates this with the "5 moons" geometry of a specific
photographed UAP (Jaime Maussan video, tweet 1934172726924894647):

"Those '5 moons' create a rotating magnetic field using 5-phase AC power, and there is
something 'magnetic and movable' inside, which the rotating magnetic field can SPIN to
make it FLY."

For 6 wire coils wired in 2 × 3 series: each group of 3 is one phase of a 3-phase supply,
producing an effective 6-pole RMF. Drew notes in tweet 2039104083945218325 (note_tweet):
"6 wire coils (given 3-phase power) that make two magnetic discs spin in opposite directions."

### 1c. XY-Plane vs. Z-Axis Field Dynamics

In conventional induction motors, the RMF lies in the XY-plane (azimuthal), coupling to
the rotor azimuthally and producing torque (azimuthal force). The Z-axis component (axial)
is nominally zero in a standard flat stator configuration.

Drew's key empirical claim: at specific "magic number" rotor-to-stator ratios, the
interaction of the RMF with the rotor's own static dipole field produces a net Z-axis force
(levitation). This is not predicted by classical induction motor theory, which yields only
torque in the XY-plane. The observation (experimental result, video
13_1927585094572630016_video.mdx):

* 3 magnets over 6 wire coils → "three magnets spinning and lifting up"
* 4 magnets over 6 wire coils → "four magnets lifting up as they spin"
* 6 magnets over 6 wire coils → "six magnets all north pole up, lifting up as they spin. The third magic number."

These are bench-scale experimental observations (claimed, not independently verified).

## 5. Homopolar Motor / Faraday Paradox — Magnetic Field Analysis

From preview file 13_1914869704390533120_preview.txt and tweet 1914870002001567817:

### 5a. Faraday Paradox (Unipolar Induction Paradox)

A conducting disc rotates with angular velocity ω in an axial magnetic field B_z. An EMF
is generated between the center and rim of the disc:

EMF = ∫₀ᴿ (v × B) · dr = ∫₀ᴿ (ωr)(B_z) dr = ½ ω B_z R²

where R is the disc radius.

The paradox: if the magnet also rotates with the disc (co-rotating magnets), is the EMF
changed? Resolution (standard physics): the B-field of a rotationally symmetric dipole is
rotationally symmetric — it does not "rotate" with the magnet. The field lines are fixed
in the lab frame regardless of magnet rotation. Therefore, EMF is generated only by the
conductor's motion relative to the fixed field lines, not by the relative motion between
conductor and magnet.

This means: spinning the magnet alone (without conductor) produces no EMF. The conductor
motion alone (relative to fixed B-field) is the active element.

### 5b. Ring Homopolar Motor Experimental Result

Video transcript 13_1914869704390533120_video.mdx: "This is a ring homopole motor and
when I add two brushes across the diameter like this, it spins."

The ring homopolar motor uses a toroidal permanent magnet (axial field) and a conducting
ring (or disc). Brush contacts at the center and rim complete the radial current path.
The Lorentz force on the radial current in the axial field produces azimuthal torque,
spinning the conductor. This is a direct application of F = IL × B where L is the radial
current path length and B is the axial field.

### 5c. Significance for Propulsion

The homopolar motor geometry is significant because:
* It produces pure torque with no commutation (no brushless switching needed after initial
  start).
* In the ring configuration, the toroidal magnet provides an axial B-field, and the
  interaction with radial currents produces spin.
* Drew's implicit claim: the homopolar geometry, properly extended to the ring/disc
  configuration with the spin/counter-spin and multi-phase modifications, provides the
  basis for UAP propulsion.
