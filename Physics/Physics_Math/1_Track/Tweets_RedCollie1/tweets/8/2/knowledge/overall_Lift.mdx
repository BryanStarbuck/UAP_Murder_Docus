## 1. Core Levitation Mechanism: Spinning Magnet in 3-Phase Rotating Field

### Configuration

* 70 mm permanent magnet (NdFeB, 14 mm central hole)
* Mounted on 8 mm stainless steel (304) vertical pole
* Three wire coils below (UVW 3-phase AC) drive the magnet to spin about the vertical (Z) axis
* Magnet spin rate achieved: up to at least 24 Hz

### Observed Z-Axis Rise

As the magnet's spin rate increases, it rises axially along the pole:
* "The magnet allows us up. Quite expected."
* "It starts spinning with three phase power to go a little faster. It rises up, popping down periodically like that."

The rise is periodic (oscillatory) in the initial configuration — the magnet rises until it contacts a mechanical stop (silicone ball on an aluminum rod) or falls back.

### Measured Upward Force

* "If we push down on the rod, we get about 500 grams before necessary to push the rod downward."
* At spin rates of 20-30 Hz: upward force on the magnet along the pole = ~500 grams (force required to prevent rise).

### Measured Net Anti-Gravity Weight (Whole Device)

* Initial measurements: "-15 grams of weight" (net scale reading reduction)
* After optimization: "-20 grams," "-30 grams"
* With counter-rotating coil assembly free to spin: "30 grams of anti-gravity weight" to "50 grams"
* Maximum reported: ~50 grams net anti-gravity for the whole device

### The 500 g vs. 30-50 g Discrepancy

The central experimental puzzle: "much of the upward spinning force of 500 grams (for this small magnet at 20-30 Hertz) seems to be cancelled by a downward reaction of some kind." The proposed explanations:
1. Newton's Third Law reaction in a direction other than axial (radial/azimuthal), but still cancelling effectively
2. Magnetic short-circuit through the paramagnetic (304 SS) rod — the rod couples the magnet's field back to the coils, creating a reaction force path
3. Missing second stage of the propulsion design (staggered coil set or lower coil)

## 2. Polarity Independence of Z-Axis Levitation

"We flip the poles of that magnet so red and north pulls up rather than the blue south. It's almost the same. The magnet will rise up to the pole and keep going down, up and down periodically like that."

Also from note_tweet 1970030336232026143: "if we place a permanent magnet on the 5 mm pole, it will spin and rise due to 3-phase magnetism (in the SAME direction, whether North-up or South-up)."

Implication: the levitation force is insensitive to the axial magnetic polarity of the permanent magnet. This rules out a simple static dipole-field gradient interaction (which would be sensitive to pole orientation) and points to a dynamic coupling mechanism driven by the rotating field symmetry.

## 3. Mechanical Stop — Separating Magnet Rise from Rod Rise

### Configuration

"We can also place a stop at the bottom so the pole can't lift up very much without lifting the entire sheet here in three wire coils. So the pole can only lift a little bit."

When the mechanical stop prevents the rod from rising, only the magnet (which is free to slide on the rod) rises within the available clearance.

### Result with Stop

"The pole of course can't lift up now. There's an upward force. But it can't lift me further than that. We see some minus weight, like minus 10 grams of gravity."

The stop converts the magnet's rise (kinematic) into a measurable static downward force on the mechanical stop — which should appear as increased weight. Instead, reduced weight is observed. This is one of the key anomalous results.

## 4. Silicone Ball Stop — Continuous Levitation Test

"First we're going to put the silicone ball closer to the magnet so that it won't jump up and down when it tries to rise. Turn on the power and you'll see the whole thing just rises up now, like that. Now we have the magnet but also the rod in the ball."

When the silicone ball (mechanical stop) is placed close to the magnet on the rod, the magnet rises smoothly without oscillating, and the rod + ball assembly rises with it. The whole compound object rises. Scale reading: "there's still not much change of weight on the scale when we do that."

This confirms: the upward force on the magnet is transmitted through the rod to the whole assembly and it physically rises — but the scale below does not register a proportional increase in total downward force. The weight of the whole device does not increase as expected.

## 5. Free-Coil Counter-Rotation — Enhanced Lift Measurement

### Configuration

The coil assembly is left free to rotate (not bolted down). The magnet spins CW; the coil assembly counter-rotates CCW.

### Result

"The magnet spins clockwise and the coil can move anti-clockwise. See how that black line has moved? And we have about 30 grams of anti-gravity weight from now. 50 grams."

The introduction of counter-rotation of the coil assembly approximately doubles the observed net anti-gravity weight (from ~15-20 g to 30-50 g). This is consistent with the spin/counter-spin hypothesis: when inner and outer elements rotate in opposite directions, the resulting field configuration produces greater net axial thrust than single-direction rotation.

## 6. XY-Plane Interactions That Produce Z-Axis Lift

The rotating magnetic field (RMF) is a purely XY-plane phenomenon: B(t) = B_0[cos(ωt) x̂ + sin(ωt) ŷ]. In isolation, an RMF in a horizontal plane exerts only azimuthal (torque) forces on a permanent magnet dipole, not axial forces.

The Z-axis lift arises from the interaction of the rotating XY-plane field with:

1. The axial field gradient ∂B_z/∂z produced by the coil geometry (real coils have axial field components that decay with distance from the coil center, creating a gradient)
2. The coupling of the spinning magnet's rotating dipole moment m(t) = m[cos(ωt) x̂ + sin(ωt) ŷ] (as the magnet spins, its moment also rotates) with ∂B_z/∂z

Force:

F_z = m · ∂B_z/∂z (z-component of force on dipole in non-uniform field)

As the magnet spins faster, the time-averaged coupling between m(t) and the axial field gradient increases — the axial force increases with spin rate. This is the physically plausible mechanism within standard EM theory, though the magnitude (500 g for a small magnet) is asserted to be anomalously large.

3. The non-linear coupling when a second counter-rotating field is added (the outer flywheel spin) — the interference of two counter-rotating fields creates a field pattern that has axial asymmetry, potentially generating a traveling wave component in the Z-direction.

## 8. Proposed Configurations for Full Levitation

### Configuration A: Staggered Dual 3-Phase Coil Sets

"In addition to these three-phase coils and magnet we have here already, we might have to add another set of three-phase coils, staggered relative to the first, some distance away."

A second set of 3-phase coils, displaced axially by a distance d and with a phase offset φ relative to the first set, creates a traveling wave magnetic field. A traveling RMF exerts a continuous axial force on the spinning magnet (linear induction motor principle). The axial force per unit length on the rotor in a linear induction motor:

F_z = (3/2) · (B_z² L r) / (s · R_r / (s · ω_s L_r))

(standard LIM thrust force formula, where s is slip, R_r is rotor resistance, L_r is rotor inductance, ω_s is synchronous speed, L and r are motor length and radius) — this would replace the oscillatory rise/fall with continuous, steady axial thrust.

### Configuration B: Lower Wire Coil

"We might have to put a wire coil below which might do something to the anti-gravity force."

A coil below the existing assembly, driven with a phase relationship to create an axial traveling wave (or to provide a Lenz repulsion from below), would contribute an additional upward force component.
