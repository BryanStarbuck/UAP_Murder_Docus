  of the flywheel).

  rotating multi-phase magnetic fields in the immediate vicinity of the craft.

## DC Disk Experiment: Anomalous Torque Transfer

### Experiment Description
A disk and a wire circuit are connected such that:
- The wires rotate in one direction when current is applied.
- The disk rotates in the OPPOSITE direction.
- The mass of the wires is insufficient by classical Newtonian mechanics to impart the
  observed counter-rotation to the disk.

Video transcription (13_2007971058230644737_video.mdx):
"Okay, I'm going to add juice to this circuit and the wires will rotate one direction and
the disc will rotate the other direction. But the wires are not enough mass to start the disc
rotating in the opposite direction, yet the disc starts rotating in the opposite direction.
Where are the torques interacting? This is not equal in opposite torques."

### Physical Interpretation
- The standard Lorentz force on current-carrying conductors in a magnetic field (F = IL × B)
  predicts that the reactive torque on the disk should equal the torque on the wires — but
  only if ALL the electromagnetic interaction is accounted for via the wire mass.
- The observation that the disk torque exceeds what can be explained by wire mass implies
  either: (a) the electromagnetic field itself carries angular momentum that is transferred
  to the disk independently of the wire mass, or (b) there is a non-Newtonian coupling
  between the rotating current and the disk.
- Drew frames this as "not equal in opposite torques" — a direct challenge to Newton's 3rd
  Law as applied to electromagnetic rotational systems.

### Relation to 3-Phase Magnetic Propulsion
- The DC disk experiment is the empirical foundation for the broader claim.
- If DC current can produce anomalous (non-Newtonian) torque transfer between circuit
  elements, then multi-phase (3-phase or 5-phase) AC driving a magnetic rotor against a
  counter-spinning flywheel may produce analogous anomalous force transfer in the Z-axis
  (lift) direction.

## 5-Phase AC vs. 3-Phase AC

Source tweet (1963381627910066488, referenced_tweet text):
"DC spin will not lift it up, just produce a strong gyroscopic motion. But if we add
5-phase [AC]..."

The note is truncated in the tweet, but the reference to 5-phase AC (rather than the more
common industrial 3-phase) suggests Drew is exploring higher-order rotating magnetic field
configurations. In standard rotating-machine theory:
- 3-phase AC produces a smoothly rotating magnetic field vector with 120-degree phase
  separation between windings.
- 5-phase AC produces a rotating field with 72-degree phase separation, enabling finer
  angular resolution of the rotating field vector and potentially higher torque density
  at low rotational speeds.
The implication is that increased phase count may be necessary to produce the anomalous
Z-axis force beyond what 3-phase achieves.

## Bob Lazar Element 115 Gravity Propulsion: Skeptical Assessment

Source tweet (2032327150901276847):
Reply to "What is your take on the gravity propulsion device described by Bob Lazar?"
Drew: "Possibly true, or possibly intel disinformation. Since a stable isotope of 115
seems now not to exist."

Element 115 (moscovium, Mc, atomic number 115) was synthesized in 2003 and officially named
in 2016. All known isotopes (Mc-287 through Mc-290) are highly unstable with half-lives
ranging from ~87 milliseconds (Mc-287) to ~650 milliseconds (Mc-290). No stable or long-lived
isotope of element 115 is known. Drew's skepticism is grounded in this nuclear physics fact:
Lazar claimed element 115 was the fuel/reactive element in a gravity amplifier, but the absence
of a stable isotope makes terrestrial fuel storage and reactor operation impossible under known
nuclear physics.

This represents Drew's only explicit engagement with conventional nuclear/gravitational
physics in this tweet set — and he approaches it skeptically rather than as an advocate.
