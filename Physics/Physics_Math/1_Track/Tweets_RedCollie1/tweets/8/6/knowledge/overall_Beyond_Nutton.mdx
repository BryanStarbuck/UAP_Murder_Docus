## 1. Core Claim: Homopolar Motor Does Not Obey Newton's Third Law

This is the central beyond-Newtonian-mechanics claim in the dataset.

Source (YAML 1966827826566623641 note_tweet): "The spin of homopolar motor does not follow Newton's 3rd law, since it is powered by a 'sideways' Lorentz force. Thus if we can make that spin-motion move slightly 'upward', it may generate an upward force, without any cancelling downward force."

Newton's Third Law states: for every action force F_AB there is an equal and opposite reaction force F_BA. In a standard motor, torque on the rotor is countered by equal and opposite torque on the stator, transmitted to the frame.

The assertion for the homopolar motor: the azimuthal Lorentz force τ = r × (J × B) on the rotating disc does not produce an equal and opposite torque transmitted mechanically to the magnet/support. The origin of this claim is that the Lorentz force is inherently perpendicular to B, meaning the field source (the permanent magnet along Z) experiences no azimuthal reaction in the standard geometry. The reaction is electromagnetic, distributed in the field, not mechanically localized to the support structure.

However, standard electrodynamics does not support this claim in the simple form stated — the Maxwell stress tensor analysis shows that the electromagnetic field carries momentum, and the total momentum of fields + matter is conserved, satisfying Newton's Third Law globally. Drew's claim is that a specific geometric configuration (spinning homopolar + 3-phase field) breaks this symmetry in practice for the forces transmitted to the support structure. This is the unresolved question.

## 3. Orientation-Independent Vortex-Lifting (Anomalous vs. Classical Expectation)

Experimental result (video 13_2010653338162061313): a permanent magnet placed in a 3-phase rotating field rises upward regardless of whether its North or South pole faces the coil assembly.

Classical magnetic force prediction: if the mechanism were simple magnetic attraction or repulsion between the test magnet and the field source, reversing the magnet orientation should reverse the force direction (attract → repel or vice versa). The test magnet should descend when reversed.

Observed result: the magnet rises in both orientations. This is presented as falsifying the magnetic-repulsion explanation and indicating a spin-dependent mechanism not captured by static field analysis.

The orientation-independence is consistent with a ponderomotive (gradient) force on the rotating magnet: the time-averaged force on a magnetic dipole in a spatially varying rotating field depends on the magnitude of the dipole-field coupling |μ · B|², not its sign. Since |μ · B|² is invariant under μ → -μ (pole reversal), the time-averaged force is orientation-independent. This is a classical physics result for rotating fields — but Drew presents it as anomalous because it cannot be explained by intuitive static-field arguments.
