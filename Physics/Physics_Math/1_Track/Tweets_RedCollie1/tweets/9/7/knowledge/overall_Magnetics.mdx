### Magnet Configurations Tested with 12-Coil Three-Phase Array

From MDX 13_2018562767792533504_video.mdx (full experimental sequence):

**Test 1 — Aluminum disc (non-magnetic induction rotor):**
- 3 mm disc aluminum, 200 mm diameter.
- Spins clockwise by simple Lenz induction (eddy-current drag in a standard three-phase induction motor configuration).

**Test 2 — 8 north-up magnets (all N-pole facing up):**
- Ring of 8 small magnets, all north-pole-up.
- Result: anti-clockwise rotation against the rotating magnetic field.

**Test 3 — 8 magnets alternating N-S (NSNSNSNS from outside):**
- 12 wire coils below with three-phase power going clockwise.
- Result: "definitely want to rotate clockwise by quarter turns. Very strong torque. This might be our best flywheel of them all."

**Test 4 — 12 magnets all N-up (NNN all the way around, like a ring magnet):**
- Result: anti-clockwise motion (same direction as for 8 N-up). Spins well "against the rotating magnetic field."

**Test 5 — 12 magnets alternating NS:**
- Result: "won't spin at all. We get no motion — pretty much cancel."

**Test 6 — 16 north-up magnets:**
- Result: clockwise rotation (opposite direction to 8 or 12 N-up arrangements). "Pretty cool isn't it? These things are very difficult to predict."

**Test 7 — 16 magnets alternating NSNSNS:**
- Result: anti-clockwise rotation (opposite to 16 N-up, same as NS for 8). Increasing speed with continued NS-NS-NS-NS drive.

The spin direction reversal between 8 N-up (anti-clockwise) and 16 N-up (clockwise), and the cancellation behavior at 12 alternating NS, reveals a complex phase-matching relationship between the number of rotor magnet poles and the 12-pole stator field. This is directly analogous to pole-count synchronism in permanent-magnet synchronous motors: the rotor pole count relative to the stator pole count determines both direction and magnitude of torque.

Extending this to a 3-phase + DC hybrid in liquid metal may produce both rotational and axial force components simultaneously.

### Magnetic Field Effects on Aluminum Disc (Lenz Levitation + Spin)

From YAML 2039863168193999003:
> "Rapid spin also greatly reduces the upward force of repulsion applied to an aluminum disc, by an AC wire coil placed just below."
> YouTube title: "AC Lenz levitation shows non-Newtonian forces, when its disc conductor is given rapid spin"

Standard Lenz levitation: an AC electromagnet beneath a conducting disc induces eddy currents in the disc; the eddy-current magnetic field opposes the applied field, producing a repulsive (upward) force. This is classical electromagnetic levitation.

Non-classical observation claimed: When the aluminum disc is given rapid spin (imposed by DC motor), the repulsion force is significantly reduced. This is presented as non-Newtonian (here meaning non-classical electromagnetic behavior). The proposed interpretation is that the spin angular momentum of the disc modifies the induction geometry — the relative velocity between the disc and the applied field is altered by the spin, changing the eddy-current pattern and thus the net force.

### XY-Plane (Horizontal) vs. Z-Axis (Vertical) Magnetic Dynamics

The 3-phase rotating field operates in the XY-plane (horizontal rotation of the field vector about the vertical Z-axis). The claimed lift (Z-axis force) emerges from the interaction of this horizontal rotating field with the spinning magnet:

- XY-plane: three-phase rotating field drives angular acceleration of the disc magnet and outer ring magnets.
- Z-axis: the gyroscopic precession of the spinning disc magnet, responding to the torque applied by the 3-phase field, generates an axial (Z-direction) force component.
- The analogy to a propeller: the propeller blade angle converts rotational motion in the XY-plane into thrust along the Z-axis by pushing fluid backward. Here, the helical magnetic field geometry provides the equivalent of blade pitch, converting spin angular momentum into Z-axis linear momentum.

The counter-spin configuration is intended to cancel the XY-plane angular momentum while preserving the Z-axis linear momentum component — the proposed mechanism for sustained net lift without net torque reaction.
