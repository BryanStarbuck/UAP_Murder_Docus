## Three-Phase Rotating Magnetic Field Drive

Source: tweet 1928356225365987388, video 13_1928355052219158530_video.mdx

Physical configuration:
* Six wire coils arranged in a three-phase (3φ) topology, winding sequence UU/VV/WW
* This is the standard stator configuration of a three-phase induction motor, which
  produces a rotating magnetic field (RMF) in the air gap
* The RMF vector rotates at the electrical supply frequency: ω_s = 2π * f_supply [rad/s]
* For a two-pole configuration, ω_mechanical = ω_electrical

Permanent magnet rotor:
* 70 mm diameter disc magnet with a 14 mm axial hole
* Mounted on a 6.3 mm aluminum aluminum rod (non-ferromagnetic -- minimal interference
  with axial field)
* 20 mm silicone ball used as a compliant mechanical interface between magnet and rod
  to transmit the upward force without rigid coupling (reduces constraint on precession)

Driving mechanism:
* The rotating stator field exerts a synchronous torque on the permanent disc magnet
* For a permanent magnet rotor, synchronous motor equations apply:
  T_em = (3/2) * (p/2) * λ_m * I_q
  where p = pole pairs, λ_m = permanent magnet flux linkage, I_q = q-axis current
* Alternatively in terms of fields:
  T = (Volume) * (M x B) where M is the magnetization of the disc and B is the stator field
* The result is spin-up of the disc magnet to angular velocities where anomalous force
  behavior is claimed to emerge

Observed frequency thresholds (experiment, video 13_1964084490902626304_video.mdx):
* 20 Hz: initial spin-up
* 25 Hz, 30 Hz, 32 Hz, 33 Hz: progressive spin-up
* 34 Hz: magnet "falls" -- the disc exits the levitation regime
  (interpreted as either resonance detuning or loss of synchronism above a critical RPM)
* The levitation window is therefore bounded: onset below ~20 Hz, collapse above ~34 Hz
  in this specific apparatus

## Homopolar Motor Magnetic Configuration

Source: tweet 1989465158549393867, note_tweet

Drive mechanism for spinning the disc magnet as a homopolar motor:
* Apply +DC voltage to a central conducting rod (axial electrode)
* Apply -DC voltage to conducting material arranged around the perimeter (rim electrode)
* Net radial current J_r flows from center to rim (or rim to center, depending on polarity)
* The disc magnet provides the static axial field B_z
* Lorentz force on current carriers: f = J x B = J_r r-hat x B_z z-hat = J_r B_z phi-hat
* The phi-hat (azimuthal) force on radial current produces torque → disc spins
* Torque: T = integral(r * f_phi dV) = integral(r * J_r * B_z * r * dr * dA)
  For uniform J_r and uniform B_z: T = B_z * I * R^2 / 2
  where I = total radial current, R = disc radius

Liquid-metal brushes:
* Conventional brush contacts cannot handle the required current density at high RPM
  without excessive wear and contact resistance
* Liquid metal (mercury, gallium, or gallium-indium eutectic) provides near-zero
  contact resistance at the sliding contact point regardless of RPM
* This is standard in homopolar generator/motor designs (e.g., Faraday disc, Bullard
  disk dynamo)

## XY-Plane vs. Z-Axis Magnetic Dynamics

The rotating magnetic field (RMF) operates primarily in the XY plane (the plane of the
disc magnet and stator coils). The proposed levitation effect is along the Z-axis
(vertical, against gravity).

The claimed mechanism for Z-axis force generation from XY-plane rotation:
* The spinning disc magnet creates a magnetic moment μ oriented axially (along Z)
  because the disc magnet is a permanent axial dipole
* As the disc spins at angular velocity ω_rotor, the gyroscopic angular momentum
  L = I_disc * ω_rotor [Z-direction]
* External torques on L cause precession rather than tilting (standard gyroscope behavior)
* The anomalous claim is that at sufficiently high ω_rotor, the gyroscopic angular
  momentum interacts with the rotating stator field in a way that produces a net Z-axis
  force without Z-axis reaction on the stator
* This requires a mechanism not present in standard classical electrodynamics +
  Newtonian mechanics (hence: beyond-Newtonian)

The ball-joint requirement:
* A rigid mount would constrain precession, converting the gyroscopic reaction torque
  into structural stress rather than upward force
* A ball joint allows the spinning magnet assembly to precess freely in all directions
* This is identified as a critical engineering detail without which the levitation effect
  cannot manifest (tweet 1945756603879469354, note_tweet):
  "I suspect that a 3-phase spinning magnet has to be attached to a 'ball joint', in
  order to capture the upward, anti-gravity force of a spinning gyroscope or top."
