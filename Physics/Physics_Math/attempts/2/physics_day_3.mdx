2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK RECAP
====================================================================

2.1 Maxwell Stress Tensor Review

The Maxwell stress tensor in vacuum is:

    T_ij = ε₀ [ E_i E_j - (1/2) δ_ij |E|² ]

The electrostatic force on a conductor (or any closed surface
enclosing charge) is obtained by integrating T over a surface S
enclosing the conductor:

    F_i = ∮_S T_ij n_j dA

where n_j is the outward unit normal to S.

Equivalently, at the conductor surface, the outward electric pressure is:

    P = ε₀ E²_n / 2

where E_n is the normal component of E at the surface. For a conductor
in electrostatic equilibrium, E is purely normal (no tangential component
on the surface), so E_n = |E| = σ / ε₀ where σ is the surface charge density.

The local pressure force per unit area on the conductor surface is:

    dF / dA = (σ² / 2ε₀) n̂ = (ε₀ E²_n / 2) n̂

This pressure is always directed outward from the conductor surface
(outward normal direction), regardless of the sign of the surface charge.
Positive and negative charge both result in outward Maxwell pressure.
This is because P ~ E² ~ σ², which is sign-invariant.


2.2 Why Asymmetric Geometry Produces Net Force

For a symmetric conductor (sphere), by symmetry:

    ∮_S P n̂ dA = 0   (all directions cancel)

For a hemisphere on a grounded plane at voltage V, the geometry breaks
spherical symmetry in two ways:

    (a) The hemispherical cap has a curved surface; the field
        concentrates near the apex (smaller local radius of curvature).
    (b) The grounded plane provides an image charge boundary condition
        that further modifies the field distribution.

The net force is:

    F_z = ∫_hemisphere (ε₀ E_n² / 2) cos(θ) dA
         + ∫_plate (ε₀ E_n² / 2) (-ẑ · n̂_plate) dA

where the plate normal points downward. The sign of the plate contribution
depends on the field geometry at the plate surface.

In the hemisphere-on-plane geometry (Day 2 result), F_z = +9.50 mN at
V = 10 kV, R = 10 cm, directed upward (+z). The hemisphere contribution
dominates and is upward; the plate contribution is downward but smaller.


2.3 Image Charge Method for Hemisphere on Grounded Plane

The hemisphere on a grounded infinite plane is solved analytically via
the image method. The conductor at potential V is equivalent to a
hemisphere with its mirror image below the plane. The potential in the
upper half-space satisfies Laplace's equation with:

    φ = V   on hemisphere surface (r = R, 0 ≤ θ ≤ π/2)
    φ = 0   on ground plane (z = 0, r > R)
    φ → 0  as r → ∞

This boundary value problem is solved via Legendre series expansion:

    φ(r, θ) = Σ_{l=0,1,2,...} [ A_l r^l + B_l r^{-(l+1)} ] P_l(cos θ)

Applying boundary conditions and using orthogonality of Legendre polynomials,
the coefficients B_l are determined uniquely (with A_l = 0 for l ≥ 1 in the
exterior problem). The surface E-field follows from E = -∇φ evaluated at r = R.


2.4 Legendre Series Convergence and Gibbs Phenomenon

The boundary condition has a step discontinuity at θ = π/2 (the edge where
the hemisphere meets the ground plane). The potential jumps from V (on the
hemisphere) to 0 (on the plane) at that angle.

Gibbs phenomenon: Fourier and Legendre series converge slowly at step
discontinuities. Near θ = π/2, the partial sum overshoots by ~9% of the
step height, regardless of truncation order L_MAX. The overshoot persists
but narrows as L_MAX increases.

Effect on force integral: The Gibbs ringing near θ = π/2 locally distorts
the computed E_n. However, the region near θ = π/2 contributes small cos(θ)
weight (cos(π/2) = 0), so the distortion in E² near the edge has minimal
effect on F_z = ∫ P cos(θ) dA. The force integral is relatively insensitive
to the series accuracy near θ = π/2.

Prediction: Increasing L_MAX from 21 to 51 and 101 should change F_z by
less than 5-10%. The Day 2 result C = 10.73 should be close to the
converged value. The true C is likely 11.0 ± 1.0 rather than 12-15 as
initially estimated from the Gibbs overshoot analysis.

3. ISOLATED CONDUCTOR PHYSICS — THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
====================================================================

3.1 Newton's Third Law and an Isolated Charge Distribution

Consider a single isolated conductor carrying net charge Q in vacuum.
The total electromagnetic momentum in the field is:

    P_EM = ε₀ ∮_V (E × B) dV = 0   (electrostatics: B = 0)

For an electrostatic system with B = 0, the momentum flux integral
reduces to the Maxwell stress surface integral over a large sphere S_∞:

    F_total = ∮_{S_∞} T_ij n_j dA → 0 as S → ∞

This appears to say the net force on the entire system (conductor + field)
is zero. But this is the force exerted by EXTERNAL sources on the system.
For a truly isolated conductor with no external fields, the self-force
integral is zero by Newton's third law (internal forces cancel).

However, this does NOT mean F = 0 for an asymmetric charged conductor.
The argument above says the total force on the conductor DUE TO EXTERNAL
FIELDS is zero. For the ground plane geometry (Day 2), the external field
is the image charge contribution. If we remove the ground plane (isolated
conductor), the image charge disappears.


3.2 Can an Isolated Asymmetric Conductor Self-Propel?

For a conductor at potential V carrying net charge Q in free space:

    The E-field outside the conductor is determined by the solution to
    Laplace's equation with boundary condition φ = V on the conductor surface.

    The Maxwell stress on the conductor surface is:

        dF/dA = (ε₀ E²_n / 2) n̂(x)

    The NET FORCE integrates this over the closed conductor surface.

Claim from classical EM: For ANY isolated conductor in the absence
of external fields, the net self-force is ZERO.

Proof sketch:

    By Green's theorem and the uniqueness theorem, the electrostatic
    potential φ outside an isolated conductor at uniform potential V is
    uniquely determined. The field E = -∇φ satisfies ∇²φ = 0 outside.

    The total force is:

        F = ∮_S (ε₀ E²_n / 2) n̂ dA

    By the divergence theorem applied to the Maxwell stress tensor
    (which has ∇ · T = ρ E for source charge density ρ), and since
    there is no free charge outside the conductor:

        ∮_S T_ij n_j dA = ∫_V (∂_j T_ij) dV = ∫_V ρ E_i dV = 0

    in the exterior region (ρ = 0 outside the conductor).

This means the integral of the Maxwell stress over ANY closed surface
surrounding the conductor (with no external charges between S and the
conductor) is zero. The net force on the isolated conductor is zero.

Conclusion: An isolated asymmetric conductor in vacuum CANNOT produce
net thrust via electrostatic pressure alone. The Buhler effect requires
an external reaction body (the ground plane or another conductor).


3.3 Physical Interpretation of the Buhler Effect

If the force on the isolated hemisphere is zero, what does the Buhler
experiment actually measure?

In the Buhler experiment:
    * The device hangs on a torsion pendulum in vacuum.
    * The electrode is at high voltage V relative to a nearby grounded surface
      (the chamber wall or a nearby plate — even at "infinite" distance,
      the vacuum chamber is at ground potential).
    * The ground plane exists even if it is remote; the image charge is
      distributed diffusely over the chamber walls.
    * The Maxwell pressure on the electrode is NOT zero because the external
      field (from image charges on chamber walls) breaks the symmetry.

For very large chamber: as R_chamber → ∞, the image charges recede
and the asymmetric force should vanish. But the asymmetric force depends
on the RATIO of hemisphere size to chamber size, not just the hemisphere
alone. The effective geometry factor C depends on the boundary conditions.

This creates a critical design question: for a spacecraft in free space
(no nearby walls), does the Buhler thrust persist?

Answer from first principles:
    * In free space far from any other conductor, the craft IS the only
      conductor. Its image charges exist only in nearby objects.
    * If the craft is truly isolated (all surfaces at the same potential),
      the net force is zero.
    * If the craft has an INTERNAL asymmetry (part at high voltage, part
      at a different voltage), then there is an internal Coulomb force
      between the two parts. But this is an internal force — it does not
      produce net momentum change for the center of mass.

Practical implication: The Buhler result measures the force on the
HIGH-VOLTAGE ELECTRODE relative to the nearby low-voltage chamber wall.
This is an external-interaction force. For free-flight propulsion, the
spacecraft must carry its own "ground plane" — either as a separate
external antenna or as a large differential in charge distribution.


3.4 Implications for the Craft Design

Two viable configurations for a self-contained craft:

OPTION A: Asymmetric internal charge — high-voltage apex, grounded base
    * The craft has a sharp apex (emitter) and a flat base (ground plane).
    * Apply HV to apex. Base is grounded internally.
    * The force is between the apex and the base — an INTERNAL force.
    * But: the base and apex are at DIFFERENT POTENTIALS within the craft.
    * In vacuum, if the two electrodes are separated by an insulating gap,
      the force on the apex is upward and the force on the base plate is
      downward, with Newton's 3rd law satisfied (they push against each other).
    * Net external force on the craft = 0. No propulsion.

OPTION B: Charge-imbalanced craft with an external ion beam
    * The craft carries net positive charge on the apex (charge +Q).
    * It emits electrons from the base (generating a net positive charge).
    * The ejected electron beam carries momentum p = mv_e in the -z direction.
    * The craft recoils in the +z direction.
    * THIS IS ION THRUSTER PHYSICS. It requires reaction mass.

OPTION C: Time-varying charge distribution (AC operation)
    * Apply oscillating voltage at frequency ω >> 1/τ_drift.
    * The time-averaged Maxwell stress may have a non-zero DC component
      if the geometry breaks time-reversal symmetry.
    * This requires a non-trivial phase relationship between voltage
      oscillation and the charge redistribution dynamics.
    * This is related to the electrostatic ratchet concept.
    * Worth investigating as a Day 4+ extension.

OPTION D: External field interaction (the tested configuration)
    * Craft as hemisphere electrode, charged to V relative to a large
      external ground (e.g., the Earth, a space tether, or a charged plate).
    * Force between craft and external ground is REAL and MEASURABLE.
    * This does produce net force on the craft in the lab.
    * For space: requires a long conducting tether to the "ground" body
      or interaction with a charged planetary surface.
    * This is an external propulsion mechanism — the reaction force is
      taken by the external conductor (ground plane, Earth, etc.).

4. CONE GEOMETRY PHYSICS
====================================================================

4.1 Why Cone Half-Angle Matters

For a conical conductor at potential V, the surface E-field scales with
the local radius of curvature ρ_c of the surface. Near the apex of a
cone with half-angle α, the apex curvature is:

    ρ_c(apex) ≈ R_apex · sin(α)

where R_apex is the apex rounding radius (for a perfectly sharp cone,
ρ_c → 0 and E → ∞). For a finite-radius rounded apex:

    E_apex ≈ V / (R_apex · f(α))

where f(α) is a geometry-dependent factor. As α → 0 (sharp needle),
E_apex increases dramatically.

The Maxwell pressure at the apex scales as:

    P_apex = ε₀ E²_apex / 2 ~ V² / f²(α)

and the apical surface area contributing to F_z scales as:

    dA_apex ~ 2π R²_apex sin(α) dα

The trade-off: sharper cones have higher E at the apex (more pressure)
but smaller area at the apex (less area to integrate). The geometry factor:

    C(α) = F_z / (ε₀ V²)

has an optimum at some intermediate α that maximizes the product E² × dA
in the z-direction.

4.2 Dimensional Argument for Optimal Cone Angle

For a cone of height h and base radius r_base, with α = arctan(r_base/h):

    * The total cone surface area ~ πr_base (slant height) = πr_base √(r_base² + h²)
    * The z-component of the normal force per unit area ~ P · cos(θ_cone)
    * At small α (sharp cone), cos(θ_cone) → 1 but E² is concentrated at apex
    * At large α (flat cone → hemisphere), cos(θ_cone) varies widely

The geometry factor C(α) is a smooth function of α with a maximum at
the angle that optimally balances field concentration against projected
area. The Day 3 cone sweep will locate this maximum numerically.

Prediction: based on the geometry arguments above, the optimum half-angle
is likely in the range 30-60 degrees. A hemisphere (α = 90 degrees) is
probably not optimal. A sharp cone (α < 20 degrees) is also unlikely to
be optimal due to limited apex area.

5. DEPARTURE FROM STANDARD PHYSICS
====================================================================

The Day 3 simulations are entirely within classical electrostatics.
No departure from Maxwell's equations or Newtonian mechanics is invoked.
The specific departure from standard expectations investigated today is:

    STANDARD: Classical EM predicts zero net force on any isolated
    charge-neutral conductor in vacuum (Newton's 3rd law for internal forces).

    BUHLER CLAIM: Nonzero net force observed in hard vacuum, even when ion
    wind is excluded. If the craft is not charge-neutral (net +Q on apex),
    some force exists but requires ejecting charge (ion thruster).

    CERNOHAJEV CONNECTION (GCD framework): Claims that the gravitational
    charge interaction allows a suitably configured charged conductor to
    interact with the local gravitational charge field and produce thrust
    WITHOUT external reaction mass. This would require new physics beyond
    classical EM. The GCD framework is not simulated today — only the
    classical Maxwell equations are used.

The Day 3 isolated conductor simulation will determine how much of the
Buhler result is explainable by the classical external-ground-plane
interaction and how much (if any) cannot be explained classically.
